Don't Ask an Apple Tree for Oranges

ARTICLE CAPTION

Keep your goal clear. Work with the conditions that can bring it about. A talk on the Diamond Sutra helped me connect aspiration, action, and a steadier mind: if you want oranges, plant an orange tree.

Apples hang from an apple tree, while a hand holds an orange up beside them. There is nothing wrong with wanting oranges. But an apple tree will not change its nature simply because you want it to. How should we pursue a goal, and how should we settle the mind while doing so? Today, I had the good fortune to visit a temple and hear a teacher speak on Chapter Three of the Diamond Sutra. The talk centered on two questions: “How should the mind abide? How should it be brought under control?” It gave me a very practical way to understand both.

My biggest takeaway was a connection I had struggled to make before. When reading the Diamond Sutra or other Buddhist texts, I often felt that their teachings made sense, yet remained separate from ordinary life. How do we actually put them into practice? That is a serious and important question.

The teacher began with those two questions about settling and guiding the mind. Put simply, I understood them as one question: when we reach what Buddhism calls the bodhisattva stage and arouse the aspiration for unsurpassed, complete awakening—when we make a great commitment—how should our mind abide? We all know the answer given in the Diamond Sutra: let the mind arise without clinging to anything.

The teacher emphasized a prerequisite: the questions follow the arousing of that aspiration. Making the commitment, asking how the mind should abide, and asking how to guide it belong together. Without making that great commitment, the question of how to settle the mind in relation to it does not arise in the same way. He also compared the three to the present continuous tense in English: an ongoing action.

Suddenly, I saw how practical this explanation could be. In ordinary life, we need not begin by asking how grand our aspiration is. We can think about a goal for the coming months or years, a plan we want to carry out. In this everyday application, that goal plays a role comparable to the great aspiration being discussed. It should, of course, be something that takes real effort.

Setting a goal today does not mean we can forget about it tomorrow or the day after. During the time we devote to what matters most, we need to bring the goal back into focus regularly. That is what I mean by refreshing it: polishing the goal or aspiration again in our mind, in our awareness. Once it becomes clear again, it reminds us of the purpose or mission of this stage. A goal kept in view can encourage us, hold us to our commitment, and move us into action.

What action should we take? That depends directly on the goal. Whatever field it belongs to, we need the relevant skills. Use what we already know; learn and investigate what we do not. In this way, the goal pulls us forward. Can you see what we have gained? A source of motivation. It prompts us to renew the goal, work out strategies, plans, and concrete steps, and consider how to reach it gradually or break it into smaller parts. This is where the explanation becomes useful in practice.

What, then, does it mean to settle the mind? Put simply, it means respecting natural laws and the actual conditions of the field in which we are working. The teacher offered an image: “Clouds in the sky; water in a vessel.” There is something entirely natural about it. Clouds move with the weather and the conditions around them. Water takes the shape of whatever vessel holds it. It neither struggles nor complains. That image offers very practical guidance as we move toward a goal or mission.

Buddhism has a familiar teaching: dependent arising and emptiness. As I understood it here, dependent arising means that different conditions come together to form what appears to be one whole; emptiness points, in this understanding, to the impermanence of things. What attitude do these ideas suggest as we pursue a goal? Whether we are working within a particular industry or considering our life more broadly, we need to recognize and respect the conditions at work, rather than force things to happen.

What does that mean? Take an extreme example: I should not demand that an apple tree produce oranges. That contradicts its nature. If I want oranges, I should plant an orange tree. Having planted an apple tree, I should not expect it to bear oranges. Whether we are working in an industry or dealing with everyday life, this approach can spare us many expectations invented by the mind, and the pull they exert on us. That pull is an important source of distress.

Can you see how this applies to the central areas of our lives—work, family, and interests? This brings me back to the idea of the present continuous. During the time we devote to our priorities, we should keep refreshing our goal or mission. At the same time, we need enough awareness to notice, at each step, stage, and small turning point, whether an unfounded expectation has appeared. We need to distinguish between what we are imposing on a situation and what follows from its actual nature. When an expectation is unfounded, we should avoid letting it pull the mind along.

One straightforward approach is to separate the situation or object from the mind, or at least create some distance between them. Then these expectations do not have to drag us wherever they go. If we can reach that point, I believe most people's lives will become more positive and their minds more stable. And when the mind becomes steadier, life as a whole becomes steadier too.

For me, this was the greatest gift of today's talk on the Diamond Sutra, and the most important guidance Buddhism offers for everyday life. What do you think?
