What Made the Buddha So Extraordinary?

ARTICLE CAPTION

A useful explanation gives you a way forward. A fixed explanation can become a trap. Building an AI agent helped me see an overlooked skill in the Buddha's teaching: making confusion legible without mistaking the map for reality.

One of the Buddha’s remarkable skills was making confusion legible. His teachings distinguished suffering, its causes, its cessation, and a path of practice. That cannot capture the whole meaning of awakening. But it offers a practical starting point: changing your situation requires seeing what you are actually experiencing.

Knowing that life is difficult leaves the next step unclear. Physical exhaustion calls for rest. Fear of losing control calls for examining what you can influence. Dependence on approval calls for reconsidering how you measure your worth. The same complaint can conceal problems requiring very different responses.

An undefined problem can feel overwhelming because you cannot see where it begins or ends. “I feel terrible” offers little direction. Distinguishing fatigue, anxiety, comparison, and uncertainty gives you something specific to examine. Naming becomes useful when it makes part of an unmanageable whole accessible to action.

Clear distinctions also reduce the cost of working together. “This project has problems” can keep a meeting circling frustration. Unclear requirements, insufficient resources, and conflicting acceptance criteria point to different decisions. Yet labels offer cheap certainty too: calling someone lazy can conceal the conditions preventing the work from moving forward.

While building an AI agent, I focused on data, answers, tools, and accuracy. Then I encountered a distinction: assisting with work, taking over work, and carrying responsibility for outcomes. I could finally locate my system. It mainly assisted. This was one useful framework, rather than a universal classification.

Over time, that distinction could change development priorities. Taking over work requires attention to complete workflows and failure recovery. Responsibility raises questions about authority, oversight, and who actually bears the consequences. Better models alone cannot settle them. A useful category reveals conditions hidden inside a vague ambition to “upgrade.”

Sometimes understanding changes before the facts do. My system and code were unchanged; a new way of organizing them revealed a direction. That also makes insight easier to share. Distinctions others can examine, try, and revise give them a route to understanding beyond accepting the speaker’s conclusion.

Reality does not have to fit our categories. An agent may span several roles; a conflict may involve interests, boundaries, and emotions together. The Buddha’s raft analogy asks us to consider what teachings help us do. I take from it a practical lesson: explanations must remain open to correction.

The causal direction can reverse. Observation initially shapes our categories; later, those categories shape what we notice and build. Seeing my system only as a question-answering assistant could keep me optimizing replies while neglecting completed workflows. Explanations influence subsequent reality, making feedback essential to revising the explanation itself.

This is what I find compelling in the Buddha’s teaching: a path for distinguishing experience, alongside a warning against clinging to its tools. A concept that becomes untouchable can create the blindness it once helped remove. Clarity means explaining what you see—and letting what you see change your explanation.

Stay curious. Keep thinking. · Wulai
