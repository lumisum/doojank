Life Is a Play. That Doesn't Make It Pretend.

ARTICLE CAPTION

Life asks us to inhabit many roles. Trouble begins when one role becomes our whole identity. Take the responsibility seriously, know when to step away, and never let a single scene become the verdict on your entire life.

There is an old Chinese saying: “When a person is near death, their words are kind.” I have been wondering why the approach of death sometimes makes speech kinder, and more truthful. The line comes from the Analects, spoken by the ailing Zengzi. It cannot describe everyone's final days. But it suggests a possibility: as life draws to a close, some people no longer feel the same need to maintain the person they appear to be.

In ordinary life, we assume there is plenty of future left. There will be another year, another decade. What we say and do today may affect our position, income, relationships, and reputation later. Before speaking, we consider more than whether something is true. Will it offend someone? Make us look incompetent? Damage an image we have spent years building? Often we know exactly what we want to say. We are also calculating what will happen if we say it.

None of this is necessarily dishonest. Social life requires roles. At work, we are accountable for what we do. With a client, we need to communicate clearly. At home, we have to consider other people's feelings. We cannot simply say everything that crosses our minds without regard for its consequences. Restraint, tact, and boundaries help us live together. The difficulty may come later: once maintaining a role becomes central to our lives, can we still distinguish what we are responsible for from what we are desperate to protect?

I think of these roles as masks. The word suggests deception, but I mean something broader: real and necessary social identities. A person can be a sincere parent, a committed manager, and a loyal friend. Each is real. Yet each mask belongs to particular relationships and circumstances. A parent's responsibilities arise in relation to their child. A manager's authority arises at work. Neither automatically extends across the person's entire life.

Wear a mask long enough, and you may forget that it can be set down. After ten years in charge, a person may begin to expect obedience wherever they go. Someone whose skills have earned them recognition may come to believe they deserve respect only while demonstrating those skills. Position, wealth, achievement, and other people's opinions begin as circumstances in our lives. Eventually, they can become the evidence we use to establish that we matter. We start relying on a role to answer the question, “Who am I?”

The shift is usually subtle. Someone says our proposal will not work, and we hear that we are not good enough. They reject a suggestion, and we feel personally disrespected. The original matter had a limited scope: one discussion, one project, one judgment. Once it becomes entangled with identity, its scope expands. A challenged opinion feels like a rejected person. The argument then turns away from improving the work and toward defending the self that has just been touched.

The end of life offers an extreme perspective on this. If someone knows their remaining future is shrinking rapidly, some things they once felt obliged to maintain may lose their importance. Next year's promotion, their reputation a decade from now, whether they won a particular argument: these may no longer seem worth their remaining time. Their family's future, unfinished responsibilities, and what they leave behind may still matter deeply. But certain concerns built around future advantage can loosen their hold.

That can make room for words previously left unsaid. Whom they loved. Whom they hurt. What they regret. Why they fought so hard over things that now seem small. Those realities were there all along, often obscured by the more urgent business of maintaining a position. Perhaps this helps explain why final words can be kind: with less need to defend an image, attention can return to actual relationships and feelings. I am tempted to add something to the old saying: their words may also become true.

Death does not automatically bring wisdom. Some people remain frightened, resentful, or determined to protect their standing until the end. Others have always been sincere and never needed a deadline to speak honestly. What interests me is the order of events. Why do we sometimes discover that something was never indispensable only when we are forced to release it? If we will eventually see the limits of our roles, could we begin to see them while there is still plenty of life ahead?

This is one way Buddhist thought has changed how I understand daily life. We establish a sense of self through countless things we call “mine”: my work, my opinions, my income, my family, my future. They concern me, but they also change with circumstances. Jobs change. Opinions can be revised. Abilities develop and decline. Other people's judgments are hardly constant. Make any one changing thing into a permanent definition of yourself, and your inner stability becomes tied to its movements.

As I understand it, the Buddhist idea of not-self does not ask us to cancel these relationships. Nor does it invite us to find an unchanging “true face” beneath the masks. We are continually shaped by experiences, relationships, and choices. The reminder is to avoid fixing ourselves into any single part of that process. What someone has done leaves consequences, certainly. But one success, one failure, one identity, or one judgment cannot exhaust what that person may become.

Seen this way, “life is a play” acquires a different meaning. The phrase often carries the weariness of someone who thinks they have seen through everything: it is all performance, so why take it seriously? Yet roles carry real relationships and obligations. Children need genuine care. Teams need sound judgment. Friends need honest treatment. Calling life a play does not turn the people in it into characters we can casually dismiss.

So life is a play, but not in the sense of pretending. I mean something closer to an excellent actor's commitment: enter a relationship and take on what it asks of you. As a parent, listen closely to your child. At work, understand the problem. If you make a promise, make a serious effort to keep it. A role has limits, but the actions within it should have real weight.

If we keep telling ourselves “this is only a role” or “everything is empty” while doing something, until we become distracted and indifferent, we have put the understanding to the wrong use. Knowing a role's limits should free more attention for the responsibility in front of us. We need not spend every moment asking what doing this task will prove about the kind of person we are. Less image management may leave more room to see what actually needs doing.

Take managing a team. The immediate questions concern progress, communication, and the distribution of responsibility. But someone preoccupied with appearing authoritative may refuse to admit what they do not know or accept a better suggestion from a colleague. Maintaining the image of a manager consumes energy needed for the manager's responsibilities. Taking the role seriously requires dropping some of the performance: ask when you do not know, revise a mistaken judgment, and accept the consequences you are responsible for.

Parenthood works similarly. Being a committed father includes guidance, protection, and necessary firmness. It also includes observing the child's actual situation. If disagreement immediately feels like a challenge to authority, helping a child can turn into demanding that the child acknowledge the parent's correctness. The more a role serves as proof of who we are, the easier it becomes to drift away from its purpose. Parental responsibility concerns the child's growth, rather than merely the parent's feeling of being obeyed.

This is where sincerity in a role begins: seeing what the relationship needs, rather than seeing only what we hope to receive from it. We often describe someone as committed when we mean they are exerting great effort. But working hard to gain recognition differs from working hard to solve a problem. Someone willing to admit uncertainty, accept correction, or give up a little visibility for the sake of better work comes closer to the seriousness I have in mind.

Knowing how to enter a role is only half the matter. Authority at work should not follow us everywhere at home. Frustration at home should not be handed to a colleague. Part of what makes life exhausting is remaining mentally inside a scene that has already ended. We sit down to dinner while replaying an afternoon argument. A child speaks to us while we are still litigating the unfairness of the day. Our bodies have entered a new relationship; our attention is still in the old one.

This is what I mean by stepping out of a role. It involves recognizing the scope of a situation again. If the afternoon argument needs further attention, arrange a suitable time to address it. It has no claim over the entire evening with your family. If someone questioned your judgment at work, examine that judgment. The people closest to you do not thereby become an audience whose respect you must win back. Roles belong to times and places. Attention can gradually learn to return to the relationship we are actually in.

The easiest mistake is to confuse stepping away with evasion. A failed project followed by “it was only a role,” with no review or repair, is no act of letting go. Responsibilities do not disappear when our mood changes. Promises remain binding even if we feel philosophically detached. What we can release is the claim that a failure defines our whole self. What still needs attention is the failure's actual consequences. Separating the two can leave us better able to deal with those consequences.

The same applies to emotion. We grieve when someone leaves, feel disappointed when an effort fails, and get angry when we are hurt. Learning a little Buddhism does not oblige those feelings to vanish. Stepping out of a role does not turn the mind into a switch. It can begin with something smaller: recognize that you are still upset, allow the feeling to be present, and decline to let it determine how you treat everyone else. The mind may need time. We can still make choices.

When I say “enter immediately, leave immediately,” I therefore mean a reminder, rather than a psychological standard that must be achieved in an instant. When a role begins, bring attention to its responsibilities as promptly as you can. When it ends, notice what you are still holding. Sometimes you can release it at once. Sometimes you need to notice it repeatedly. At least the direction is clear: being unable to leave a scene does not mean the scene deserves control of the rest of your life.

A deeper difficulty arises in our judgments of ourselves. A failed project turns “this went badly” into “I am a failure.” Success turns “this worked” into “I must always be a successful person.” The conclusions seem opposite, but both fix one period of life into an identity. The first makes it difficult to start again; the second makes it difficult to admit mistakes. One past scene becomes a script every future scene must follow.

Recognizing this can make reflection clearer. Where was the judgment wrong? What preparation was missing? Which conditions were outside our control? What could change next time? These questions point toward something practical. They are more useful than repeatedly putting our worth on trial. Success can be examined in the same way: which methods helped, which results depended on timing or other people's contribution, and which conditions cannot yet be reproduced? The achievement remains real without having to guarantee who we are forever.

None of this requires a person to become someone entirely new every day. Experience accumulates. Relationships continue. Promises take time to fulfill. A good father does not lose his responsibilities on leaving the house. A responsible employee cannot invoke the end of the workday to cancel an obligation already accepted. Stepping out changes how we allocate attention and identify ourselves. Continuing obligations continue. A particular posture, emotion, or form of authority simply need not invade situations where it does not belong.

I used to describe the ideal as “one hundred percent commitment inside the role, zero attachment outside it.” On reflection, it works better as a direction than as a description of anyone's actual state. No sentence can dissolve every attachment. Time and energy are limited; no one can perform every role perfectly. The useful practice is to act wholeheartedly when action is needed, accept adjustment when it is needed, and stop extending a scene indefinitely when it is time to leave. Serious commitment does not require becoming a perfect person first.

The Buddhist teaching often translated as “non-abiding” becomes concrete here. We still work, have identities, pursue results, and cherish relationships. The mind need not remain permanently lodged in one identity, one judgment, or one outcome. Respond when something arises. Complete the necessary follow-through. When conditions change, let judgment and action change with them. Releasing a fixed attachment and taking effective action can happen together.

Returning to the beginning, my understanding of death has also changed. Death ends our ability to participate in these roles; it does not necessarily release the mind's grip on them. Practice lets us examine that grip while action is still possible. What is genuinely mine to take responsibility for? Where am I refusing to yield merely to preserve an image? What still deserves effort? Which scenes am I replaying long after the replay has ceased to help anyone? We need not wait for the final moment to make those distinctions.

The thought that final words may become kind and true can then move from a distant reflection to a present reminder. Some thanks can be expressed now. Some apologies can be offered now. Some arguments can be interrupted long enough to ask what is still being fought over. The future needs planning. Yet if managing the future keeps postponing the relationships available to us today, our regrets may grow from precisely the things we always assumed there would be time for.

Thinking of life as a play does not diminish its weight. Knowing that family relationships change gives us reason to be present today. Knowing that a position can end gives us reason to carry its current responsibilities well. Knowing that each period of life has an ending means we need not mortgage our entire existence to its wins and losses. Enter sincerely while the role is there. Adjust when it changes. When it ends, make room for the life that follows.

This is the sense in which life is a play without being pretend. Enter sincerely, and leave promptly. Promptly means less needless delay, not less responsibility or feeling. We need neither watch coldly from outside the theater nor remain trapped onstage proving ourselves. Take the performance seriously, but do not let any single scene become the verdict on your entire life. Perhaps that is the value of practice: we need not wait until every role has ended. We can begin to see while we are still alive.

Stay curious. Keep thinking. · Wulai
