AI Has Attention Now. Ours Has Scattered.

ARTICLE CAPTION

AI creates endless images. Your attention is finite. Are you watching videos—or chasing novelty? Through Buddhist non-clinging and Wang Yangming’s view of intention, reclaim your attention for what truly matters.

When we scroll through videos, the easiest thing to overlook is what we are doing to our own minds.

It may begin as a few minutes of rest. Something interesting appears, so we stop. The next one is more interesting, so we watch a little longer. By the time the phone is finally put down, a good deal of time has passed — and the things we meant to do have not become any easier. Sometimes we want to do them even less. We would rather watch a little more.

Lately, seeing more and more AI-generated video has made me more concerned about this. Animals talk. Historical figures walk into modern life. Things that could never happen are made to look as if we had seen them with our own eyes. The images grow stranger, the stories more unexpected, and there is almost always a new scene waiting.

I used to understand the problem this way: video scatters the mind, so scroll less and return to the present moment. That is not wrong, but it is only half of it. The sharper question is this: when appearances can be manufactured without limit, where will the human mind be led?

It Is Not Appearances We Chase, but New Ones

When we used to speak of clinging to appearances, it was easy to picture attachment to one definite thing: seeing a better life and craving that life, seeing wealth and craving wealth. The object was clear, and the mind held on to it.

Short video has produced a stranger condition. A person no longer even has time to cling to a single appearance — what he clings to is the next one. A talking animal passes in two seconds. A historical emperor riding the subway passes. A city on Mars passes. We treasure none of these images. All we want is: show me one I have not seen before.

So what truly keeps us from stopping is not any particular video. It is novelty itself. The finger keeps changing the picture, while the mind stays fixed on a single demand: keep me feeling something new — do not let me stop.

What makes this so sharp today is that the cost of making appearances has changed. In the past, manufacturing a new appearance in the real world was expensive: travel, filming, actors, sets, effects, editing. Behind one startling image stood a great deal of other people's time and money. Generative AI has driven that cost steeply downward, and something fundamental has changed with it: for the first time, the supply of appearances far exceeds what a human being can take in.

A day is still twenty-four hours. A person's attention is still finite. But appearances are now close to infinite.

What will be scarce in the future is not content. It is human attention.

Wherever Intention Is, There Is a Thing

In the Chuanxilu — the recorded conversations of Wang Yangming, known in English as Instructions for Practical Living — Wang says: "The mind is the master of the body. What the mind sends forth is intention. The substance of intention is knowledge. Wherever intention is directed, there is a thing."

Set beside our subject, this sentence could hardly be more to the point: wherever your intention settles, there your world is being formed. In Wang's usage, a "thing" is not only a physical object. It is whatever intention is engaged with — and it takes shape only because intention keeps returning to it.

Suppose an evening leaves you two free hours. If your intention rests on a book, those two hours become understanding. If it rests on your child, they become a stretch of relationship between you. If it rests on code, they become a system. If it rests on music, they may slowly become a piece of music. Things are not simply lying there finished; intention, placed on them again and again, is what makes them grow.

But if intention is cut into twenty-second pieces, fifteen-second pieces, leaping among dozens or hundreds of unrelated objects, it forms nothing at all. Many images are seen; nothing grows under your gaze.

This is now my deepest worry about scrolling. Its greatest loss is not wasted time. It is that intention can no longer rest, for any length of time, on anything that truly matters. A skill, a relationship, an essay, an undertaking, a practice — none of these grows from a single glance. Each needs a person to keep placing intention there, over time, before it slowly takes shape.

Strictly speaking, what each of us has in a day is not merely twenty-four hours of time. It is one finite portion of intention. Where you place it, there your life grows.

Placing Intention Is Not Clinging

Here a question has to be faced directly. Buddhism teaches non-clinging; Wang Yangming teaches that intention must have somewhere to rest. Is placing intention on one thing for a long time not also a form of clinging?

The difference lies in whether the intention is your own to command. Clinging to appearances means intention is no longer yours: you want to stop and cannot; there is nothing you actually want to watch, and the finger keeps swiping. The mind is being led away by appearances. Placing intention is the opposite: I choose to put my intention here, I engage with it fully — and when the time comes, I can take it back. One is being carried off. The other is setting something down, freely.

The Diamond Sutra says: "All appearances are illusory." And it says: "Let the mind arise without dwelling on anything." Non-dwelling was never a command to do nothing and enter nothing. In the sutra, the Buddha puts on his robe, takes his bowl, goes into the city to beg for food, returns, eats, puts away robe and bowl, washes his feet, and sits down to rest — each thing done completely. The tasks are ordinary; the intention is wholly present in each one.

I once wrote about a woman I worked with who loved to talk — except when she was driving on the highway, when she grew noticeably quiet, because at highway speed the one thing that mattered was driving well. Her intention was entirely on the road. It was not coldness toward her passengers. She knew where her intention belonged in that moment.

Here the two lines join. Buddhism reminds us not to hand the mind over. Wang Yangming tells us where the mind should be put to work. Inwardly, not led away by the ten thousand appearances; outwardly, still doing things in earnest. We may look, we may know, we may appreciate — but we do not have to chase. Let the image pass, and place intention back on what we have truly chosen.

Who Governs Your Attention?

One more change deserves to be stated plainly. In 2017, the paper that introduced the Transformer was titled Attention Is All You Need. Much of AI's astonishing progress rests on this mechanism of attention: teaching machines, amid a flood of information, to judge what deserves to be noticed.

Machines are getting better at allocating attention. Human beings are getting worse at it. To be precise, it is not the Transformer itself that takes our attention. What completes the taking is three things working together: generative models producing appearances without limit, recommendation systems selecting and delivering them, and product design built to hold us. But the result stands before us:

AI creates intelligence out of attention. We hand our attention over to AI.

So the final question is not how many videos you watched today. Ten or twenty, in themselves, amount to little. The real question is: who governs your attention? Does every new appearance that shows up automatically lead it away? Or do you know clearly where your intention belongs — and can you keep it there?

A person who knows his own mind is not forbidden to watch videos. The difference is only this: watching is his choice, and stopping is his choice too. However new, however strange the appearance, his one thought does not follow it away.

Keep This One Thought for What You Have Truly Chosen

Return to the opening scene. Next time you pick up the phone, there is no need to scold yourself, and no need to vow to quit entirely. Add only one observation: when the thought "just one more" arises, look first at what this mind is actually waiting for. Is it waiting for something specific, something genuinely worth watching? Or is it only waiting for the next appearance it has not seen before?

If it is the latter, practice putting the phone down, and let intention return to something you have already chosen. Finish the meal. Read the passage through. Hear the person out. Or go back to the work that needs long investment and shows no result yet. Every while that intention stays there, that "thing" grows a little more.

Appearances can now be generated without end. This one thought of mine will not be given away so lightly.

Stay curious. Keep thinking. · Wulai
