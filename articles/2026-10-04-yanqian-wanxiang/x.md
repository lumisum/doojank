# Endless Images, A Mind That Won’t Let Go

When we scroll through videos, it is easy to overlook what we are doing with our own minds.

Perhaps we begin with a few minutes of rest. Something interesting appears, so we stop. The next clip is more interesting, so we stay a little longer. By the time we put the phone down, a fair amount of time has passed. The things we meant to do have not become easier. Sometimes we feel even less willing to begin them. We would rather watch a little more.

Seeing more AI-generated videos has made me think about this more carefully. Animals speak, historical figures enter modern life, and impossible events look as though we witnessed them ourselves. The scenes become stranger, the stories more surprising. There is almost always something new waiting.

My view is simple: we should spend less time scrolling through ordinary videos, and be especially careful with AI videos that can keep inventing new scenes. By scrolling, I mean watching without a clear purpose, moving from one clip to the next. Good explanations, genuine records, and worthwhile creative work have value. The value of the content and the way we consume it still deserve separate consideration.

Through the lens of Buddhist practice, the question I want to ask is this: as more appearances pass before us, are we understanding more clearly, or becoming easier to pull along?

## 1. The images change. The mind keeps waiting.

The videos keep changing. A landscape gives way to an argument, then to a life we envy. The pictures and emotions shift. It seems as though we never settle on anything.

Look more closely, though, and the mind may remain inside the same expectation: will the next one be more interesting?

We want more of what we like, skip what we dislike, and replace what bores us. Our fingers make many choices, but those choices often serve a single demand: keep me interested; do not let this end.

This has made the Buddhist idea of clinging more concrete for me. Clinging does not require staring at the same image for a long time. The image can change while the mind holds tightly to the demand for another moment of satisfaction. We may not be attached to any particular clip, yet remain attached to the pursuit of the next one.

**The finger changes the image. The mind keeps waiting for its next satisfaction.**

Encountering many appearances does not automatically mean clinging to them. Reading, working, and walking also bring us into contact with many things. What matters is how the mind responds: can an experience pass? Can we stop when it is time? Or do we have nothing in particular left to watch, yet still find it difficult to finish?

If every moment of boredom is immediately filled with a new image, there is little opportunity to understand that boredom. Where does it come from? Does the body need rest? Is the task difficult? Have we become accustomed to constant stimulation? Before these questions have time to emerge, another video arrives.

We may end up seeing more while noticing less of our own minds.

## 2. AI can keep producing images. Our days do not get longer.

Making a video has always depended on conditions. People have to appear, scenes have to be arranged, and filming and editing take time. Animation and visual effects could create imaginary worlds long before AI. What AI changes is how easily many once-demanding images can be produced, and how quickly unusual scenes can follow one another.

Things rarely seen in everyday life can become ordinary on a screen. If one talking animal is no longer interesting, another can take its place. When an improbable story ends, another can be generated. The content keeps renewing itself, but the person watching still has only so much time in a day.

My concern extends beyond whether a video is real. A scene can attract us even when we know it is invented. It never happened, yet it awakens curiosity, surprise, liking, or dislike. Knowing an image was generated does not automatically free us from its pull.

The Diamond Sutra teaches that appearances are deceptive. That teaching reaches far beyond spotting fabricated videos; it cannot be reduced to a test of whether footage is genuine. Still, images that can be generated and replaced so easily prompt a useful question for me: if this appearance depends on so many conditions and changes so readily, why do I keep asking it to satisfy me?

Even the extraordinary can become familiar. After one astonishing scene, something similar may no longer feel new. The content offers further variation, and we keep looking for it. Without choosing to stop, we may watch until fatigue, a work obligation, or an empty battery ends the session for us.

A screen can always offer another clip. It cannot decide whether that clip deserves our time.

This is why restraint matters especially with AI videos. They can bring more worthwhile creativity, and also more stimulation we do not need to take in. Content being available does not oblige us to watch it. One interesting scene does not mean the next hour belongs to the feed.

## 3. Each time we leave, something in front of us remains unfinished.

I previously wrote about a colleague who drove us on a trip. She usually enjoyed talking, but became noticeably quiet whenever the car entered the highway. Her explanation was straightforward: the most important thing then was to drive safely. Conversation would divide her attention, so she concentrated on driving.

That experience brought me back to the opening chapter of the Diamond Sutra. The Buddha puts on his robe, takes his bowl, goes out to collect alms, returns to eat, puts away his robe and bowl, washes his feet, and sits down. Before the teaching unfolds, ordinary life proceeds one activity at a time.

I find a simple reminder in this: each moment has something that calls for our attention. Eating, walking, and washing each involve a process we can enter fully. The activity may be ordinary; the care we bring to it can still be real.

Heavy scrolling can crowd out that care. We watch while eating, reach for the phone when work becomes difficult, and glance at the screen while a family member speaks. Each interruption seems brief, but the activity before us is interrupted repeatedly, and repeatedly we must find our way back.

We may also be rehearsing a way of responding: a little boredom, and we switch; a little effort, and we leave; a little discomfort, and we cover it with something new.

If this response carries into daily life, a passage that requires thought can seem too slow. Listening to someone finish can seem too long. A task without an immediate result becomes something to put aside. Content on a screen can be replaced instantly. Much of life becomes intelligible only when we stay with it.

Videos do not explain every difficulty with attention. Fatigue, pressure, and the difficulty of a task all matter. But we can still ask whether we habitually leave even at moments when we could have engaged.

**How we use our attention now also helps shape how we meet the next moment.**

The Buddhist understanding of causes and consequences can be considered close to home. Careful reading creates an opportunity for understanding. Patient listening creates an opportunity to see a relationship more clearly. Conversely, repeatedly switching away from difficulty may make switching away feel more natural next time. Many conditions influence the result, but the way we participate is itself one of those conditions.

## 4. Filling the silence does not necessarily resolve what is inside it.

Someone might reasonably ask: work is exhausting enough, so what is wrong with watching something that requires little thought?

We need rest. We do not have to be learning constantly or turning every minute into output. Yet the way we rest also deserves observation. After watching, have the body and mind loosened? Or have the images stopped while the mind still wants more, so that even a quiet moment feels uncomfortable?

Sometimes fatigue is only part of what we want to escape. A problem remains unclear, a disappointment has not been addressed, or something feels difficult to face. Videos draw our attention elsewhere and offer temporary relief. When the screen goes dark, the original situation remains.

If each moment of unease is met with new content, that unease rarely has a chance to be understood. We search for an appearance that will make us comfortable without looking closely at what we fear or hold on to.

This is where non-clinging feels particularly practical to me. It does not require a world without images or a mind without feelings. When irritation arises, we can notice irritation. When boredom appears, we can look at boredom. When the urge to pick up the phone arrives, we can recognize the urge. Noticing a thought does not require immediately acting on it.

Even a small pause can create a little more room to choose.

Non-clinging also cannot excuse unlimited viewing simply because we tell ourselves we are not attached. Whether we are chasing stimulation, whether the body is tired, and whether responsibilities are being neglected need to be faced honestly. Setting a boundary around viewing, and reducing content that repeatedly draws us beyond it, are part of taking responsibility.

Compassion can include ourselves. A long scrolling session need not become an immediate accusation of weak willpower. We can first notice how the habit develops, make stopping easier, and adjust gradually. Practice can involve both seriousness and patience.

## 5. Watch one less. Give this moment a chance to be whole.

I now think of scrolling less as a concrete practice. It need not begin with quitting completely, or proving ourselves more aware than anyone else. Recovering the ability to end one session deliberately already matters.

Before opening the feed, ask: what do I want to watch? If there is something specific, watch it. If opening the app is simply a reflex, acknowledge the search for a little stimulation. While watching, occasionally notice what the content has given you, and whether you still want to give it more time.

When you are ready to stop, the thought worth observing is precisely this: just one more. It may be faint or powerful, but it is still a thought arising now. There is no need to satisfy it immediately. Put the phone down first, and let what is in front of you come back into view.

No grand plan is required. Finish the meal, understand the passage, hear your family member out, or simply sit quietly for a while. Try staying with one ordinary activity until it is complete.

The Diamond Sutra's teaching of bringing forth the mind without clinging to anything offers a reminder here: we can loosen our grip on the next stimulus and still participate wholeheartedly in life. Once the videos stop, there are people, responsibilities, and our own bodies and minds deserving attention.

This applies to ordinary videos, and gives us even more reason to be careful with AI-generated ones. Technology can produce ever more appearances. We need to become clearer about which deserve a place in our lives, and which we can encounter without pursuing.

There will always be more images than we can finish watching. What keeps us scrolling is often the thought within us that does not want the experience to end.

The next time that thought appears, perhaps wait before swiping. Notice what the mind is waiting for, and give this moment a chance to be whole.
