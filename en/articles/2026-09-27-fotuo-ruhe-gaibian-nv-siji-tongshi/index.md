A few days ago, several of us drove to Hangzhou for a data summit. A female colleague was at the wheel.

She is normally very talkative. There is plenty to discuss at the office and on the road: work, children, whatever life brings up. On ordinary city roads, even driving herself did not stop the conversation.

But I noticed a curious change. As soon as we entered the expressway, it was as if someone had pressed mute. She stopped starting conversations and kept her eyes on the road. When we spoke to her, she answered briefly. Once we left the expressway, she resumed talking as though the quiet person had never been there.

*On the expressway, safety comes first: our colleague concentrates on driving.*

At first I thought it might be coincidence. On the return journey, it happened again: quiet on the expressway, talkative afterward. Why would someone who loved conversation change so completely?

Those days I had been wondering how Buddhism relates to ordinary life. We read the Diamond Sutra about non-clinging and the Heart Sutra about form and emptiness. We learn not to let thoughts drag us around. How does that enter a person's work, family, and daily choices? Practice surely cannot begin when we open a scripture and end when we close it.

My colleague had studied Buddhism for more than ten years. On the outward journey I asked how it had affected her actual behavior and judgment. She did not give me much of an answer then.

After the meeting, we returned to the expressway and she became quiet again. I finally asked why. Her answer was simple: on an expressway, safety comes first. The important thing is to drive well. Conversation divides attention; higher speeds leave less room for error. So she tries to give driving her full attention.

Then she mentioned the opening of the Diamond Sutra, which describes the Buddha dressing, taking his bowl, entering Śrāvastī to seek alms, returning, eating, putting away his robe and bowl, washing his feet, and sitting down.

I had always wondered why such an important scripture began with ordinary details instead of its famous teachings about appearances and non-clinging. That evening in the moving car, I began to feel that their ordinariness was precisely what deserved attention.

When the Buddha sought alms, he sought alms. When he ate, he ate. Afterward he put things away and washed his feet. Only then did he sit down to teach. None of these acts was skipped because a great scripture was about to be taught; none became unimportant because he was the Buddha.

![Before the teaching began, the Buddha's ordinary life had already expressed it](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-27-fotuo-ruhe-gaibian-nv-siji-tongshi/images/02.png)

*Before the teaching began, the Buddha's ordinary life had already expressed it.*

Before the formal teaching, his life had already shown a way of being. Living in the present may be more concrete than a slogan about relaxing. It need not forbid remembering the past or planning the future. **First give proper attention to what this moment calls for.**

On the expressway, drive. When it is time to talk, talk. When it is time to plan, plan carefully. Concentration means keeping irrelevant concerns from constantly pulling the mind away, rather than never looking beyond the immediate moment.

Our exhaustion often comes not from a task's complexity, but from carrying too many things while doing it. The body handles today's work while the mind races into tomorrow, then worries about other people's opinions. Before one thing ends, the next has already entered. One task is open, but the mind has a dozen windows running.

The Buddha's ordinary sequence feels remarkably uncluttered by comparison. Do what is needed; when it is done, move to the next thing. Every completed event need not continue happening inside the mind.

This connects with non-clinging. The Buddha still dressed, ate, walked, and sought alms. The practical tasks remained. The difference may be this: **enter fully into the task while it is happening, and stop holding it when it has passed.**

![For now, drive well: steady hands guide the car through pine-green hills](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-27-fotuo-ruhe-gaibian-nv-siji-tongshi/images/03.png)

*For now, drive well: steady hands guide the car through pine-green hills.*

Looking back, I think my colleague had answered my original question. I asked how Buddhism enters life. She offered no elaborate theory; she simply stopped chatting on the expressway. At that moment, she was the driver, and her responsibility was to drive well.

This experience does not exhaust the meaning of the Diamond Sutra's opening. A scripture is richer than one personal interpretation. But it helped me realize that practice need not be an extra activity added outside life.

If Buddhist teaching enters life, it should appear in the least conspicuous places: eating, driving, listening, completing a task, and treating family members. We need not wait for a major crisis to remember it. The mind can begin changing within these small acts.

I used to wonder how Buddhism might solve life's big problems. Now I suspect that many big problems grow from countless moments we have not attended to well.

The Buddha had not yet begun his discourse. His life had already offered a teaching.
