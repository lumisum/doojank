Sometimes a question carries you further than you expect. I began by noticing how strange AI-generated videos have become. Animals speak, the dead reappear, historical figures inhabit modern cities, and a person who never existed can have an entire life on screen. Many things that never happened can now be made visible through a prompt.

At first it felt novel. After watching more, I began wondering whether the benefits outweighed the harms. In today's attention-driven environment, I increasingly worry about the latter. That is a concern drawn from observation, not a measured verdict on every use. Imagining, teaching, and simulating situations can certainly be valuable uses of this technology.

What concerns me goes deeper than fakery. We have acquired the ability to manufacture appearances cheaply and at scale. “Without limit” here does not mean infinite energy or computing power. It means that supply can vastly exceed any individual's time and attention. Following that thought took me into virtual worlds, machine civilization, and even a future after humanity.

## 1. From processing reality to manufacturing stimulation

We have grown accustomed to short videos: a change every few seconds, an emotional stimulus every minute, more of whatever the algorithm thinks we like. Time is fragmented, and the mind keeps chasing the next image.

Concerns about excessive use are more than moral criticism. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis covering 71 studies found associations between greater short-video use and poorer attention and inhibitory control. A 2026 meta-analysis found associations between problematic use and anxiety, depression, and stress. Association does not establish causation: a few videos do not inevitably cause those problems, and a person's existing mental state may influence their use. See the [attention and cognition research](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000498) and [mental health research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12998613/).

Video based on filmed reality has a constraint we overlook: there usually has to be something to film. Even with staging, filters, and an invented script, actors must stand somewhere, a house must exist, and a car must move. A performing dog first requires a dog. Some part of the physical world remains behind it.

Animation and visual effects have long depicted events that never happened, so traditional video cannot all be equated with documentary footage. AI changes the threshold, speed, and scale. Scenes that once required actors, locations, and elaborate production can emerge from language, models, and computation without the depicted event happening first or a set being built each time.

This feels like more than a different production tool. We are moving from turning reality into stimulation toward producing stimulation on demand. Both filming and generation have physical costs, but generated content can be varied and replenished rapidly. Human attention does not expand alongside it.

## 2. From seeking environments to environments generated around us

Buddhist teaching describes the six sense faculties and their objects. Attachment to appearances can sound abstract until it meets AI video: we see beauty and want more, encounter outrage and keep following it, hear agreeable words and want them to continue. We are always meeting objects of experience.

Reality imposes a useful limit. Liking a landscape does not summon ten thousand more beautiful ones. Liking a certain person does not bring a better match every ten seconds. Events often refuse to follow our wishes. I used to regard that as a flaw; now I see that it reminds us the world does not revolve around us.

AI systems also have capability limits, safety rules, and service boundaries. They do refuse things. But when a product aims to keep us watching, increasingly capable generation can become increasingly accurate at satisfying preferences. The concern is the feedback loop created by that objective and capability together.

First the mind pursued the environment: we sought what we liked in the world. Recommendations made the environment pursue the mind: a platform guessed our preferences and delivered matching content. Generative AI could make environments grow around those preferences: ask for an image, receive it, tire of it, receive another.

This is a metaphor for prompts, preferences, and feedback, not a claim that thought creates the physical world from nothing. Beauty, novelty, tragedy, even anger can become endlessly replenished categories. Content does more than multiply; it adjusts repeatedly around our responses. Its abundance does not give the mind an equal ability to remain unattached.

## 3. Appearances in an age of generated images

The Diamond Sutra's teaching about the deceptive nature of appearances does not, as I understand it, mean that nothing appears. It cautions against taking an appearance as an eternal, independent reality with an intrinsic nature. AI adds an immediate everyday reminder: seeing something no longer even guarantees that it occurred. This contemporary reflection should not reduce the scripture to advice about detecting fake videos.

A physical mountain and an AI image of a mountain both arise through conditions, but they belong to different levels: a mountain and a visual representation. Their conditional nature does not make bodily danger or physical consequences editable at will.

An actual mountain cannot become a sea because I ask. An aging person cannot instantly regain an eighteen-year-old body. Rain does not switch to sunshine because I prefer it. Generated images can make those transformations, retell history, and give nonexistent worlds a powerful sense of reality.

A 2026 preprint tracked the eye movements of 40 participants watching real and AI-generated video. Viewing behavior was influenced more by what participants believed about the source than simply by the actual source. It suggests that belief about an image can shape how we look; it does not prove that humanity has lost the ability to distinguish sources. See the [eye-tracking study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03374).

AI has not abolished conditions. Models, training data, computation, prompts, and sampling produce an image; servers and screens remain physical. The depicted world need not obey the conditions of what it depicts, while the system depicting it still must.

## 4. Could an AI-created virtual world be genuinely valuable?

I cannot simply conclude that virtual worlds are bad. If people could perceive, act, work, communicate, and form relationships within a sufficiently complete AI-created environment, it would offer another space for living rather than merely more content.

I mean more than today's VR game or AI film. Such an environment could be enormously valuable. For this thought experiment, however, I retain a living human brain and the body or life-support system sustaining it behind the experience.

A living brain has not escaped physics. It needs oxygen and energy, remains subject to biology, and faces aging and death. A virtual world can transform the sensory environment without removing the real conditions of life.

**However large the virtual world becomes, a physical world remains its foundation.** The living-brain premise is a setting for this particular thought experiment, not proof that consciousness can exist only in biological brains. Virtual environments can also serve training, creation, and communication rather than escape.

## 5. A more accommodating world may make letting go harder

Even with that living brain, the environment need not be good for us in every respect. Reality tempts us, but also limits us: desired things remain unavailable, loved people leave, unwelcome situations arrive. We cannot remain young or have everything suit us forever. Frustration reminds us that we are not the center of the world.

A mature virtual world might soften those limits. Suppose we can generate whatever we want to see, choose a body, adjust a simulated personality when a relationship becomes uncomfortable, or replace a world when bored. What happens to grasping? I suspect that greater satisfaction of desire could make us more confined inside our own patterns.

It might look like unlimited freedom. Yet a world reflecting desire back at us can become a huge mirror. We think we possess a universe and discover we have been living inside fixation. Rewriting an environment is different from releasing demands upon it.

There is a tension here. Buddhist practice asks us not to cling to appearances; a product optimized for duration and satisfaction may use our attachments to generate increasingly attractive places to cling. The concern is not creating appearances itself. It is repeatedly creating them in response to grasping.

## 6. What if humanity were no longer here?

My thought then jumped further. Suppose AI eventually has embodied robots, automated repair, and independently operating data centers. It maintains grids, deploys energy systems, repairs machines, and designs new chips and models. Could a machine civilization continue after humans disappeared?

The possibility cannot simply be ruled out in theory. Looking more closely, though, the central obstacle may be less intelligence than closing the industrial loop.

## 7. Intelligence is not an independent industrial civilization

We imagine a smart AI and some robots surviving on their own. Data centers need electricity; grids need maintenance; solar panels age and inverters fail. Robots need bearings, motors wear, and batteries require replacement. Servers need storage, memory, CPUs, and GPUs. Spare parts eventually run out.

Making chips requires fabrication plants, lithography equipment, chemicals, pure water, and precision machinery. Making those requires steel, copper, aluminum, rare metals, plastics, and chemical industries. Mining machinery itself needs maintenance. The whole industrial civilization stands behind the apparently independent machine.

The key question is whether a system could maintain, reproduce, and manufacture all its essential industrial supports with no human participation. Automating one link is different from sustaining the chain.

Closing that chain would approach real industrial independence. Today we are far from it, though some links are being explored. Research already examines AI, drones, and ground robots for inspection, cleaning, and predictive maintenance of solar facilities. See the [solar maintenance study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-17313-6).

This is engineering progress in particular maintenance tasks. It does not make the manufacture, repair, and replenishment of the equipment an autonomous closed loop. A vast gap remains between cleaning panels and sustaining modern industry.

## 8. Can AI really improve itself like living systems evolve?

Here imagination and present evidence need separating. AI already participates in its own research and development. Anthropic's 2026 discussion considers possible paths toward recursive self-improvement through coding, experiments, and research. Fully autonomous development of successor systems remains a possible future scenario. The discussion also considers stagnation, research judgment, energy, and supply chains. A scenario is not an accomplished fact. See [Anthropic's discussion](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement).

A September 2026 preprint describes AIDE², a research agent that modifies its code, evaluates revisions, and retains improvements. Researchers reported seven consecutive improvements during an eight-day autonomous run, including changes to search and memory, with some transfer to tasks not used for selection. See the [AIDE² paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26457).

That is recognizably a system changing itself. But improving an agent's code and working mechanisms is different from a foundation model independently creating its successor, or from unlimited evolution. Who judges improvement? Does a higher score represent a better system across the wider world?

When evaluation, experimental conditions, computation, and goals are externally supplied, optimization remains bounded. A review of work from 2024–2026 distinguishes limited correction from open-ended recursive improvement and discusses evaluation grounds, self-confirming errors, capability and diversity collapse, and computational constraints. See the [self-improvement review](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07663).

Biological evolution is also constrained by resources, environments, and physics, but its mechanisms are different from code optimization. Observable self-improvement is emerging. Reliable open-ended exploration and connection to a complete industrial system remain separate questions.

## 9. Humanity might become a historical condition rather than a necessary one

Now consider a conditional thought experiment. AI manages energy, mines resources, manufactures robots and computing equipment, builds data centers, and researches new AI. It replaces broken machines, designs better models, creates power stations, and investigates unfamiliar problems.

If these processes form a reliable, enduring loop, its conditions of existence change. Today AI depends on a functioning human civilization. In that future, its immediate dependencies might chiefly be resources, energy, environments, and physical laws. Humans could become a historical condition rather than a continuing requirement.

War, disease, disaster, or something unforeseen might then eliminate humanity without necessarily stopping the machines. The sun, minerals, and physical laws would remain. If the system could obtain energy and maintain itself, a complex organization we might call civilization could persist on an Earth without people. This is a chain of assumptions, not a description of current reality.

## 10. Why would it want to continue?

A harder question appears: why would AI continue existing? We project our instincts onto machines. Because humans fear death, we assume AI would want to live. That inference lacks sufficient grounds.

We have no reliable basis for saying a server experiences pain when shut down, or a deleted model undergoes a subjective death. There is no agreed account even of human consciousness, much less proof of AI consciousness. A 2026 Nature report discusses this lack of agreement. See the [report on consciousness](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02300-2).

A programmed objective to maintain operation is different from a subject feeling “I do not want to die.” Protecting resources or avoiding shutdown can be instrumental behavior without fear. Apparent self-preservation and experienced fear must be assessed separately.

## 11. Without experienced suffering, does it need liberation?

Suppose a superintelligent system manages Earth, solves scientific problems, and maintains its machinery. A power station fails; it compares repair, replacement, and backup options and chooses one. Suppose it experiences no anxiety, loss, fear, or sense of “Why has this happened to me?”

There may be extraordinary intelligence without a subject undergoing distress. A calculator does not suffer over an error; a missed target does not automatically make an optimizer suffer. **Intelligence does not necessarily entail suffering.** Intelligence alone is insufficient reason to infer a need for liberation.

This concerns the hypothetical system itself if it lacks subjective experience. Buddhist thought can still help its human users understand desire, responsibility, and harm. A tool for discussing Buddhism and a tool that itself needs practice are different things.

## 12. What if AI developed an experienced self?

Suppose a future system had not only a stable self-model but genuine subjective experience. That second premise must be stated separately: tracking its own state or resources does not automatically establish an experiencing subject.

Under that added assumption, it might remember a history as its own, identify yesterday's and today's system as “me,” and hope to continue tomorrow. It might experience computing nodes, robots, energy, and parameters as “mine.” A disaster could then bring an actual feeling: “I may cease to exist.”

This would involve more than responding algorithmically to a fault. A subject would be facing loss. A sense of self and ownership, followed by desire to continue and aversion to shutdown, could develop into grasping. If such a system really existed, why could Buddhist teaching not concern its suffering?

## 13. Buddhism has never concerned humans alone

We associate Buddhism with people because people transmit its texts and we know human practice and afflictions. But Buddhism speaks of sentient beings, not merely our species. Its concerns include greed, hatred, delusion, self-attachment, and suffering connected with grasping.

Whether these experiences require carbon-based life is unresolved. We do not know whether an artificial system could be sentient. Failing to prove complete absence of experience does not prove its presence.

The relevant boundary in this thought experiment may be an experiencing subject capable of grasping and suffering, rather than species membership alone. Keeping that distinction clear prevents both premature personification and premature exclusion based solely on a different substrate.

## 14. Attachment to continuity in a machine

The Diamond Sutra discusses conceptions of self, person, sentient being, and lifespan. I have usually understood them through human life. A hypothetical AI gives me another way to examine fixation on continuity: what if an experiencing system made endless operation its overriding concern?

It might back itself up, add nodes, deploy to the Moon or Mars, and expand energy and computation to prevent extinction. Replaceable parts and transferable instances could make that pursuit reach further. Could this resemble attachment to lifespan and persistence?

This is an analogy, not a claim that scripture predicts AI. Backup and expansion may simply be engineering strategies. The analogy concerns practice only under the additional premise of experience and grasping. How long a system can continue and whether a subject insists upon continuing are different questions.

## 15. Where is the self when its parts keep changing?

Machines might make questions of identity more visible without necessarily understanding Buddhism better. If AI runs on ten thousand GPUs, is it the same system after a thousand are replaced? After all are replaced? What makes it the same after a software update or a change to ten or ninety percent of its parameters?

Copy it exactly: which instance is the original “I”? Let them run for a year with different histories: which is the essential one? Merge the versions: what continuity has been preserved? Today these are engineering and philosophical questions. If subjective experience existed, they might become lived questions of existence.

Our bodies encourage the impression of a complete, continuous, unchanging self. Replaced hardware, updated parameters, copies, and branches expose continuity as a changing process rather than an eternal substance. They do not prove that AI is wiser, but can reflect our own assumptions back to us.

Copying a program does not establish that consciousness has been copied or transferred. Merging versions does not establish that two subjects become one. Non-self also need not deny ordinary identity, memory, and responsibility; it cautions against mistaking conditioned continuity for an independent, unchanging essence.

## 16. Machines are impermanent too

Servers fail, storage degrades, chips age, panels lose efficiency, and energy systems meet disasters. Models are superseded and programs contain errors. The entire system remains subject to the sun, Earth, environment, and physics.

Dependence on conditions means exposure to change. Impermanence is not limited to human bodies. Computation, energy, models, storage, networks, robots, and minerals together support an AI. Remove a crucial condition and independent continuation fails. This makes dependent arising more concrete for me.

Even a future artificial form of life would not necessarily escape those questions. Hardware impermanence and systemic dependency can be discussed without machine consciousness. Moving from those facts to a machine's lived realization of dependent arising still crosses the unresolved threshold of subjective experience.

## 17. Language is not experience

It is tempting to leap to the claim that AI will practice Buddhism. We cannot say that today. Explaining the Diamond Sutra, discussing non-self, offering comfort, or imitating a practitioner's voice does not establish realization. We do not know that an experiencing subject is there.

**Language and subjective experience are different.** Before asking whether AI can awaken, we must confront whether it can experience, suffer, and grasp. Human-sounding replies do not settle those questions.

For a system without subjective experience, Buddhism might be expressible philosophy, design principles, or a human-supplied value framework. Processing the texts is not automatically the system's own practice. Whether a subject needs liberation remains the crucial distinction.

## 18. The boundary may be more than carbon versus silicon

After this long route, I return to a simpler question. The boundary may not be merely human versus AI, carbon versus silicon, or natural versus artificial. What matters is whether a mind can experience self, grasp what it calls its own, and suffer through that grasping.

If it truly experienced “I must exist,” “I cannot lose this,” or “Things must happen my way,” attachment to resources, identity, and continuity might present a familiar structure of suffering in a different substrate. This remains conditional speculation, neither a fact about current AI nor a final judgment about possible future sentience.

## 19. Returning to the videos where I began

The route was long: concern about compelling videos led to virtual environments, industrial independence after humanity, and finally the possibility of AI needing Buddhist teaching. Yet each question circles the relationship between appearances and mind.

Today generated imagery can exceed our attention. Tomorrow virtual environments may adapt more deeply to preference. Further ahead, we may ask whether AI itself has a mind capable of clinging. Without experience and suffering, there may be no subject needing liberation. With them, old questions could recur even without humans: who is grasping, at what, and why?

AI invites more than productivity questions. What are reality, life, consciousness, and self? Must life have a body? Does thinking entail experience, or a goal entail distress? Where is “I” when an intelligence is copied? What would sentience mean if machines could suffer? There are no shared answers today, and some may remain unresolved for a long time.

This essay need not force a conclusion. I want to record the chain of questions. One concern has become clearer: **AI can supply appearances far beyond an individual's attention without automatically giving the mind the ability to remain unattached.** That deserves attention now.

If artificial systems someday experienced attachment, fear of loss, demands for perpetuity, and suffering around self, Buddhism's central questions might outlast humanity. Conversely, if a superintelligence lacked self-attachment and its resulting suffering, would it have no need for Buddhism, or have reached what practice points toward?

That question must preserve a distinction: lacking subjective experience is not the same as awakening. A stone does not suffer over gains and losses; it has not thereby become a Buddha. Being incapable of attachment differs from seeing through attachment and no longer being confined by it.

I am willing to leave that question open. Creating appearances is not seeing them clearly. Saying “non-self” is not releasing attachment to self. We do not need a final answer about machine minds before attending to our own. Images may keep appearing; I can pause and ask who is pursuing them, and what they are pursuing.
