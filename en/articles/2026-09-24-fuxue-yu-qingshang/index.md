I have been thinking about a question: if someone develops a real grounding in Buddhist thought, will their emotional intelligence improve too?

By emotional intelligence, I do not mean becoming smoother with words, better at reading a room, or more skilled at pleasing people. I mean something else: being less easily provoked by a single remark, less desperate to prove oneself, and more willing to understand another person's feelings and circumstances. If that change is possible, Buddhism has a very direct connection with everyday life.

We usually discuss emotional intelligence as a collection of techniques: leave room in what you say, do not retaliate immediately, hear the other person out, and consider their perspective. Those techniques help. The difficulty is that most of us already know them; we cannot always put them into practice when something actually happens.

Someone criticizes you in public and shifts their own responsibility onto you. You work hard to accomplish something, only to hear it dismissed in a sentence. Everyone knows they should stay calm, but what rises first is often: “Why should I accept this?” or “I need to explain myself so nobody thinks it was my fault.”

Increasingly, I feel that the hardest part is in the mind rather than the technique. Why can another person's words set us alight? Often they have touched something we call “me”: my dignity, my judgment, my interests, my position, or my picture of myself.

Seen this way, the Buddhist idea of non-clinging becomes quite ordinary. The other person finished speaking an hour ago, but you are still thinking about it. A day has passed, yet you replay the scene in bed and wish you had answered differently. The person has gone, the words have faded, the event is over. Only the mind is still staying there. Perhaps that is one form of clinging.

Non-clinging does not mean having no emotions or forcing them down. It means not having to hold on to them indefinitely. We can recognize anger, hurt, or sadness without immediately allowing it to make our decisions. That is where the teaching meets emotional steadiness.

A steady person may not simply be unusually good at enduring things. Some experiences no longer settle so deeply in their mind. They notice praise and criticism, but neither needs to become the whole measure of their worth.

Then there is compassion. We often say that emotional intelligence requires empathy. The real test of empathy comes when someone is hurting us, rather than treating us kindly.

Why does someone dominate, control, belittle, or shift blame? Such behavior can be genuinely unpleasant, and understanding it does not make it right. Looking one layer deeper, however, can reveal something else. Forcefulness may conceal a fear of losing control; boasting may express a desperate need for reassurance; repeated attacks may come from persistent anxiety and insecurity.

Compassion is not making excuses or abandoning boundaries. It is seeing a little more than the behavior alone. “How could they treat me like this?” may then be joined by another question: “What is making them act this way?” That small opening is already close to what I understand as emotional intelligence.

Many conflicts stop being about solving a problem and become a way of passing emotions along. Someone is humiliated at work and snaps at their child. A manager criticizes them, and they lose patience with a colleague. A couple argues over something small until neither remembers what started it. Each thinks they are fighting back, while pain is simply being handed to the next person.

A more mature emotional intelligence may include refusing to be that relay point. I can feel another person's anxiety without throwing it at someone else. I can protect myself from harm without needing to hurt them in return just to make them feel it too.

If all this relies on suppression, it is difficult to sustain. Suppression itself takes energy. A deeper steadiness may emerge as something inside changes: some victories matter less, some judgments no longer get under our skin so easily, and some words can actually pass.

This brings me to attachment to the self. The more heavily we invest in “me,” the more easily a situation turns into conflict. Disagreeing with my view feels like rejecting me. Someone else's success seems to prove my inadequacy. A person departing from my expectations feels like an insult. We may be defending a self that constantly needs vindication instead of dealing with the matter before us.

Emotional intelligence training says, “Do not argue back right now.” Buddhist inquiry can ask, “Why am I so eager to argue?” The first says, “Do not worry so much about what others think.” The second asks, “What is this self, so afraid of judgment, trying to hold on to?” One offers a way to act; the other helps us examine why acting that way is so difficult.

Buddhist study may therefore change the mind beneath emotional intelligence rather than directly teach conversational skill. Non-clinging makes a remark less imprisoning. Compassion helps us see beyond our own position. Wisdom reminds me that my first reaction may not be the whole story. Noticing attachment to self can loosen the insistence that I must compete, win, and prove myself.

In daily life, other people may simply say, “That person has good emotional intelligence.” Yet the change may have happened in their mind, rather than in their way with words.

Does Buddhist knowledge improve emotional intelligence automatically? I do not think so. But when someone gradually brings what they learn into their relationships and choices, emotional intelligence may be a natural result.

Emotional intelligence unfolds between people. Its roots may have been in our own minds all along.
