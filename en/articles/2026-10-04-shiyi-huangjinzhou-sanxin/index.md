When China's National Day Golden Week arrives, people head out. Some seek mountains, some the sea, some an old town or a few days in a hotel. They go despite crowded highways and packed attractions. Asked why, many give the same answer: “To clear my mind.”

That used to sound entirely natural to me. Work is tiring, life is frustrating, and a change of place, scenery, and food can make us feel lighter. Recently I began wondering: **when we say we want to clear our minds, what are we trying to clear?**

I am not against travel. After long stretches of work, the body needs rest, and repetitive surroundings can be exhausting. Mountains, water, unfamiliar cities, and good food can interrupt a familiar rhythm. Changing our surroundings helps. But does it change the mind, or mainly what the mind is encountering?

At the office, a computer brings work to mind; a WeChat message suggests another task. At home, homework and chores activate another familiar set of feelings. On holiday, those cues give way to mountains, seas, grasslands, temples, and old towns. Familiar pressures recede and new impressions arrive. Of course we feel relief.

What happens a few days later? The holiday ends, the suitcase returns home, the alarm rings, and we sit at the same desk. The computer, work, and homework remain. The familiar mind often returns with them.

There may even be a new comparison: recently I was feeling the sea breeze, and now I am back in the office; recently I woke naturally, and now I am rushing. “It was better when I was away,” the mind says.

![A change of scenery offers a pause: a traveler rests among mountains and the sea](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-10-04-shiyi-huangjinzhou-sanxin/images/01.png)

*A change of scenery offers a pause: a traveler rests among mountains and the sea.*

I began to distinguish **using surroundings to unwind** from **depending on surroundings for peace**. The first is ordinary: one setting feels stressful, so we enter another and relax. The shift worth noticing is the belief that peace can only be found elsewhere.

Only there can I feel good. Only by leaving this life can I relax. Only in a temple can I be quiet. Only among mountains and water can I find myself. It still looks like clearing the mind, but we are beginning to hand the mind over to circumstances.

I feel irritated, so I must leave; unsettled, so I must visit a temple; anxious, so I must find silence; distressed, so something must take the distress away. We keep searching for the next place, trip, or holiday. A helpful condition for rest gradually becomes the only support we trust for inner peace.

**If the mind can be peaceful only in a particular setting, who governs it: us, or the setting?** This helps me understand the Diamond Sutra's teaching of bringing forth the mind without clinging to anything.

What once sounded like a distant spiritual state now feels practical. Clinging means the mind grips something: this place feels good, so I want to stay; that place hurts, so I want to escape; gaining brings joy, losing brings sorrow. We demand that agreeable conditions remain and disagreeable ones disappear. The mind keeps being pulled by things outside it.

Buddhist practice invites me to examine how I hold mountains, cities, work, and family. I put it into a phrase for myself: **See circumstances as empty of a fixed essence; see the mind the same way.** This is my learning note, not a newly coined scriptural quotation.

![The mind need not be handed over to its surroundings: return to an ordinary desk with care](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-10-04-shiyi-huangjinzhou-sanxin/images/02.png)

*The mind need not be handed over to its surroundings: return to an ordinary desk with care.*

Mountains, seas, and offices still exist. Closing our eyes does not remove the world. Seeing circumstances as empty means recognizing their conditions and change: no state can be held forever. A trip ends, a holiday ends, distress may shift too. Something unbearable today may look different years later. Why require one changing circumstance to guarantee our happiness permanently?

That is not enough, because we can begin clinging to our own inner states. I must be serene, peaceful, never troubled. Today feels good; tomorrow must feel the same. We used to cling to the outer setting, and now cling to the inner mind. The habit may be unchanged.

So the mind, too, deserves observation. Notice distress when it arises, quiet when it appears, anger and joy when they arrive. Do not rush to expel one or grip the other. Inner states are also conditioned and changing. None has to become a version of ourselves we must own forever.

Read this way, non-clinging does not require abandoning travel, work, or life. Travel when appropriate, visit mountains or temples, eat, and return to work after the holiday. Gradually stop asking these activities to secure the entire mind for you.

![Circumstances change, and so does the mind: tea and pine shadows beside a quiet window](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-10-04-shiyi-huangjinzhou-sanxin/images/03.png)

*Circumstances change, and so does the mind: tea and pine shadows beside a quiet window.*

With mountains, enjoy mountains; without them, sit at the desk. Enjoy the holiday without giving work complete ownership of the mind afterward. When favorable conditions come, loosen the grip. When difficult ones come, see them clearly and decide how to respond, rather than being left only with an urge to flee.

Perhaps another kind of clearing the mind becomes possible: not scattering it across Yunnan, Tibet, a beach, or a temple, but ceasing to pin it tightly to any one place.

**True freedom is not finally finding a place where I feel at ease. It is no longer needing one particular place in order to feel at ease.**

That is why I am not against traveling to clear the mind during Golden Week. Quite the opposite: when it is time to go, go. Travel often gives us **a few days of relief**; the deeper work of practice is to help us gradually find **an ease that can accompany us through life**.

**Travel changes our surroundings for a few days. Practice changes our relationship with surroundings over a lifetime.**

Set out this Golden Week. After seeing the landscapes, perhaps ask one more question: **If I did not have to go anywhere, could I still let this mind settle?**
