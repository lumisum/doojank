I increasingly think this may be one of the most practical moments for ordinary people to engage seriously with philosophy. Not because the world has suddenly become more literary, or because everyone needs to study Kant, Hegel, or Buddhism. It is because AI is getting so capable. Work that once required considerable time can sometimes be advanced by explaining a need, then reviewing and improving the result. As execution becomes more accessible, our choices carry more weight.

AI already helps us write code, analyze information, organize research, develop plans, and investigate scientific questions. It still makes mistakes, and its performance varies considerably across tasks. Yet a change is visible: more cognitive work that we once had to do ourselves can now be carried forward with its assistance. If more mature general intelligence emerges, the change could extend further, from interpreting a request and breaking it into tasks to executing, checking, and revising the work.

One thing seems worth understanding early: proving that you are smarter than AI does not have to be a condition for using it. You do not need to remember more, write faster, or produce a more comprehensive analysis before you are entitled to its help. Learning and expertise still matter. You need them to assess results, identify mistakes, and understand consequences. But the purpose of learning can shift toward understanding problems more clearly and collaborating more effectively with the tool.

As our ability to solve problems grows, another question becomes harder to avoid: what deserves to become a problem in the first place? Imagine asking a very capable AI system to help you make more money. It might research industries, analyze markets, compare strategies, and develop an extensive plan. Before adopting that plan, you still need to consider why you want more money, how much would be enough, and what you would give up to get it. If your income rises while your health, relationships, and freedom diminish, is that still your version of success?

There is no universal answer. One person wants a higher income to escape financial pressure. Another wants to prove something about themselves. Someone else sees other people accumulating more and feels unable to stop. The stated objective looks the same, but the reasons differ. Without examining those reasons, smooth execution can turn a vague impulse into a very real way of life.

AI can help us find out how to do something. It can also discuss why we might do it, what we should pursue, and where our values contradict one another. I do not want to reduce it to a machine that understands methods but cannot engage with values. The distinction that matters to me is this: however persuasive its advice becomes, we still live through the choices we make. Consequences for other people do not disappear because “AI recommended it.” Assistance with judgment cannot become an excuse to abandon scrutiny or responsibility.

This is where philosophy becomes practical. Much of ordinary life involves dealing with work, income, housing, family, and education. Our energy goes into getting things done. Questions about who we are, what is good, or what constitutes a worthwhile life can seem remote when urgent tasks remain unfinished. But as AI helps us handle more of those tasks, a question previously hidden by busyness becomes more visible: if I can do increasingly many things, where should I put my finite life?

By engaging with philosophy, I do not mean memorizing the history of ideas. I mean finding ideas that speak to your experience, understanding them over time, putting them into practice, and testing them. Some people find guidance in Stoicism about what they can change. Others turn to existentialism to understand choice and responsibility. Still others learn from Confucianism, Daoism, or Buddhism about how to live in relation to the world. Eventually, those ideas should help shape how you judge actual choices.

But a philosophy that fits you cannot simply be a collection of ideas that makes you comfortable. If it always defends my habits, excuses me from considering other people's feelings, and protects me from admitting mistakes, it resembles a language of self-protection. A philosophy worth relying on should help me stand on firmer ground while allowing me to revise my position. It should ask why I believe something and whether that belief survives contact with reality.

Reading many books does not automatically produce that ability. AI can explain philosophical concepts, compare traditions, and help us examine a difficult question more deeply. There is little reason to turn philosophy into another knowledge contest with a machine. What matters is whether these ideas enter your life: how you choose when interests conflict, how you respond when a plan breaks down, and whether you can still hear another person when they disagree with you.

A simple distinction keeps returning to me: explaining an experience and living through it are different activities. Someone can describe change beautifully while being unable to accept a change in their own career. They can discuss death while avoiding the recognition that they, too, will die. They can analyze suffering and then discover, when genuinely hurt, that the explanation has not yet become a capacity they can draw on. Life tests whether an idea remains language or has begun to change how we respond.

Likewise, an AI's ability to explain suffering does not establish that it experiences suffering as a person does. Its ability to discuss death does not establish that it confronts mortality in the human sense. Whether machines can have subjective experience is a separate, contested question; I do not need to settle it here. The immediate lesson for me is that even an excellent explanation cannot substitute for my own practice. AI may describe a path clearly. I still have to walk it to discover what I have understood.

I once took this thought in another direction. If AI was getting stronger intellectually, perhaps people should cultivate wisdom through philosophy to preserve an advantage over it. Eventually I realized that I was still imagining a competition: the machine would be clever, and I would be clever on some higher level. Changing the name of the contest did not change my need to prove superiority.

That contest is unnecessary. We do not need to outrun cars or outcalculate calculators. AI is much more complex than either: it can help propose goals and organize steps, so these comparisons have limits. Still, they offer a useful starting attitude. I can focus on using it well, checking its work, deciding when to trust it, and recognizing when to take a decision back into my own hands. I do not have to keep demonstrating that I am better than it.

**Philosophy helps me use increasingly powerful intelligence while remembering why I am using it.** This does not mean believing that a single instruction gives me absolute control over a complex system. It means being able to express a need, recognize a deviation, revise a plan, and stop when an apparently efficient solution conflicts with something I genuinely care about.

Most goals are more complicated than they sound. “Improve efficiency” raises questions about whose efficiency improves, which burdens diminish, and whether someone else's burden increases. “Help my child grow” requires a view of growth: better scores, independent judgment, or some balance between them. “Get more people to read my writing” raises another question: how far am I willing to change it for attention, and what would I refuse to say even if it attracted more clicks? An impressive result can still be a result I should not accept.

When defining a task for AI, I therefore need to explain what I want, what must not be sacrificed, how a good result will be recognized, and when the effort should stop. The system may still fail to interpret or implement those requirements, so review remains necessary. But if I have never considered them myself, I am more likely to confuse the easiest thing to measure with the whole purpose. Income, speed, and audience size have value. None automatically represents a good life.

This is why value judgment matters so much to me. Prompting matters too; unclear communication affects results. Even if AI becomes better at helping us improve our prompts, however, someone still needs to examine whether the request is worth making. Greater precision does not make a mistaken direction right. Execution can shorten the journey to a destination we want. It can also get us faster to a place we later wish we had not chosen.

Two people with similar AI tools may make different choices if one asks only how to obtain more while the other also asks what that “more” would actually give them. The second person might not earn more and is not automatically more virtuous. But they have a better chance of noticing whether their pursuit matches the life they want. This does not erase differences in resources, skills, or circumstances. It does remind me that the user's judgment matters alongside the model's capabilities.

Your philosophy becomes visible in such judgments. What is a need, and what is a desire created by comparison? Which goal have you chosen seriously, and which has survived only through habit? What costs can you accept, and which relationships should not be casually traded away? When should you persist, and when are you continuing because you cannot bear to let past effort go? Without examining these questions, social expectations, platform recommendations, or AI suggestions can quietly arrange your priorities for you.

Of course, nobody can settle every question of value before beginning to live. I do not believe there is a philosophy that can deliver a certain answer to every choice. Circumstances change, values conflict, and commitments may need revision. A stable system of judgment need not consist of unchanging conclusions. It can be a way of examining new situations carefully. It should let me say “I don't know yet” and change my mind when new facts become visible.

Nor can it concern only my own comfort. We live in relationships. One person's improved efficiency may affect a colleague's work. A family's decision may reshape a child's life. A successful plan may transfer risk to somebody else. If my philosophy amounts only to “whatever benefits me,” it will not help me see the full cost. Responsibility belongs in the direction we choose; it cannot simply be added after a tool has achieved the result.

I think we can begin with one immediate task, without first finding a perfect philosophical system. Before delegating it to AI, describe the situation you hope to change. Ask where the goal came from and how achieving it would change your life. Invite the AI to propose different paths and identify assumptions, tradeoffs, and possible blind spots. Then consider which suggestions deserve an attempt and which you are not ready to accept.

Afterward, look back. Did efficiency actually improve? Was the problem you cared about resolved? Did a new burden appear? If a plan keeps meeting its metrics while drifting away from its purpose, revise the metrics rather than accelerating for their sake. Philosophy is close at hand in this process: choices are tested against reality, and judgment becomes more reliable over time.

This gives me a more ordinary understanding of wisdom. It does not need to be a mysterious attainment or a badge of human superiority. It can mean recognizing what you still do not understand, distinguishing capability from purpose, acknowledging costs, and changing direction when necessary. AI can participate in that process, revealing possibilities and contradictions we had missed. Growth depends partly on our willingness to face what comes to light.

That is why I increasingly believe that stronger AI makes a personal philosophy more important. We do not have to become philosophers before using powerful tools. But as our capabilities expand, we should take the grounds for our choices more seriously. In the past, an unexamined impulse might remain an idea because execution was difficult. In the future, it may more readily become a plan and an action. Greater capability brings opportunity and makes examining direction more necessary.

**Delegate more of your work to AI without delegating your reasons for living.** AI can offer a daily expansion of information, opportunities, strategies, and possibilities. Without your own criteria for choosing, you may appear increasingly capable while becoming less certain why you are so busy. An expanded field of action does not require you to travel every available path. Being able to do something does not establish that it deserves doing.

I no longer think of philosophy as a way to distance myself from AI. I see it as ongoing preparation for collaborating with powerful intelligence: learning to accept, question, and decline its advice. AI can help me explore destinations, compare routes, and move forward. Throughout that process, I need to consider which choices deserve the time, relationships, and life I will commit to them.

Human wisdom and AI intelligence can work together. The former needs learning and revision; the latter needs constraints and review. Neither needs to be idealized, and they need not become opponents. What I want is for the work to improve while my choices about life become clearer.

When AI asks, “What should we do next?”, I do not need an immediate, grand answer. I hope I can at least respond thoughtfully: what is worth doing here, why it matters, and which costs I am willing to accept. If I have not worked that out, I can pause. Increasingly powerful AI should give us more choices. A philosophy of our own helps us decide what to do with them.
