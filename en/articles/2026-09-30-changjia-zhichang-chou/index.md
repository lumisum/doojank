The National Day holiday has arrived, and it is time to rest. Yet leaving the desk does not necessarily stop work inside the mind. Why did I miss the bonus? Why was my achievement overlooked? The harder I try to relax, the more those grievances return.

After years at work, I have often felt unable to make a breakthrough. Two lines in the Platform Sutra recently helped me see that part of what confines me is in my own mind.

The verse links self-attachment with fault and speaks of the incomparable blessing of forgetting one's merit. In the text, Fada has recited the Lotus Sutra three thousand times. When he bows before the Sixth Patriarch, his head does not reach the ground. The Patriarch notices his pride and challenges it. Recitation was practice; the thought “I have recited it three thousand times” had become a credential for elevating himself.

I recognize something similar when a task succeeds. “Surely there should be an extra reward this time.” When no reward appears, the achievement loses its shine. Remembering my effort produces resentment. Even success can become the beginning of distress.

My salary arrives regularly and my benefits are stable. Still, I expect extra material recognition for doing more or doing better. Sometimes I even wait for that recognition before feeling motivated. My motivation ends up in another person's hands.

There is nothing shameful about working for money. What hurts is turning an unagreed reward into an account receivable in my mind. I think the company owes me, though it may know nothing about that debt.

![Record your contribution without carrying it as a burden: a worker examines a notebook](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-30-changjia-zhichang-chou/images/01.png)

*Record your contribution without carrying it as a burden: a worker examines a notebook.*

At work, people can cooperate well when discussing actual problems and tasks. Organizational changes, performance rankings, raises, and bonuses often change the mood. The work is the same, but attention has shifted from the matter itself to our position within it. Who is valued? Who receives more? Does my achievement count? Am I losing out? A shared problem becomes a basis for comparison.

Changes in material interests do affect life. Alongside them, however, I carry an expectation: because I did these things, the company should give me that outcome. When it does not, past achievement starts to hurt.

An employee and a company may also measure contribution differently. I see difficult problems solved, pressure carried, and hours invested. The company may focus on revenue, cost savings, risk reduction, and alignment with its immediate priorities.

What I consider exceptional may be considered part of the role. A problem I regard as crucial may not change the outcome the company cares most about. Merit in my eyes does not automatically count as additional merit in theirs.

The company need not be right, and my effort does not lose its value. But my assessment will not automatically become theirs or turn into a bonus. Without clear standards, we may keep discussing two different things.

Self-attachment becomes concrete here: I judge merit by my own measure, then expect others to repay me by it. I expect shared rewards after shared pressure, forgetting that the latter does not automatically create an agreement about the former.

The original verse addresses attachment to self and pride. I find in it a way to examine attachment to repayment. The heavier the “I,” the harder the work feels.

Forgetting merit suggests another possibility. **Contributions can be recorded and used when discussing compensation without being carried constantly inside the mind.** Releasing the urgent need for recognition can make present decisions clearer.

I also find a personal connection with the Daoist phrase about following what is naturally so. Work and home have different conditions and ways of functioning.

Work involves responsibilities, outcomes, evaluation, and distribution of benefits. Clarifying objectives, doing the work, and discussing contributions and compensation explicitly belong to that relationship. A company having a different measure of value is one of its conditions.

Family has another way. Feelings, companionship, and everyday stability have their own weight. Performance measures from work do not belong everywhere at home. Affection should not become a ledger of who contributed more and who owes whom.

When rankings, comparisons, and disappointments begin governing how we speak to family, workplace distress is reproduced at home. A company's judgment does not extend over my whole life. Recognition missing at work cannot be extracted as compensation from those close to me.

![Work belongs to work; life belongs to life: a briefcase stays by the door as a family gathers](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-30-changjia-zhichang-chou/images/02.png)

*Work belongs to work; life belongs to life: a briefcase stays by the door as a family gathers.*

I use “following what is naturally so” here to mean meeting each relationship according to its actual conditions. Do the job at work; outside it, step back from its comparisons. Work matters should be addressed within work, and family life within its own relationships.

Unkept promises and unfair arrangements still need to be confronted. Cultivating the mind can help me choose clearly instead of using resentment as a substitute for a decision.

The same pattern extends beyond work. Parents give and expect their child to develop according to their hopes. Friends help and expect comparable help. Partners carry more and expect a response. Expectations are natural. “I gave, therefore you must produce my chosen outcome” can nevertheless turn generosity into a debt that pulls a relationship around.

Different relationships can share the same attachment: using our contribution to determine how another person should respond.

Forgetting merit gives me room to loosen that insistence. Returns can be discussed, promises should be honored, and an unequal relationship can be adjusted. I need not repeatedly use past effort to force present reality into my imagined shape.

**Giving has its value; repayment has its conditions.** Seeing both helps us decide how to remain involved.

I return, finally, to the Diamond Sutra's teaching of bringing forth the mind without clinging. In these situations, not being confined by credit, recognition, and reward is how I understand non-clinging. Continuing to act, care, accept responsibility, and choose is how I understand bringing forth the mind.

![The mind can let go while the work continues: a quiet morning by a window and an open book](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-30-changjia-zhichang-chou/images/03.png)

*The mind can let go while the work continues: a quiet morning by a window and an open book.*

The Platform Sutra illuminates attachment after giving; the Diamond Sutra lets a mind that has released attachment remain active. That is what most moves me: **the mind can let go, and the work can still be done.**

This holiday, let the body rest, and perhaps the mind too. Notice the merit you are carrying and whose reply you are waiting for. Loosen that burden, then act more freely.
