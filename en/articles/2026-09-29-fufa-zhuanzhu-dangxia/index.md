A few days ago, my child's school assigned a project on Lu Xun's *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk*. Instead of writing a few sentences, the students were to express their understanding of the author and the book through words and pictures. It was an interesting task: read, understand, and turn that understanding into an expression of one's own.

My child's first response was to find a similar project online and redraw it almost exactly. It could certainly be handed in. The assignment looked finished, yet something felt wrong to me.

Eventually I understood that the issue was not drawing skill or the mere use of a reference. The child had bypassed almost everything that made the task valuable. The task was completed without really being entered into.

*Copying accurately is not the same as understanding: a child pauses before a book and blank page.*

I thought again of the Diamond Sutra's opening. At mealtime, the Buddha dressed, took his bowl, entered the city for alms, returned, ate, put away his things, washed his feet, and sat down.

These details seem ordinary. No great theory or mysterious state has yet been described. But perhaps this ordinariness is one of the easiest parts of Buddhist practice to overlook.

When eating, eat properly; when walking, walk properly; when washing, wash properly. Let the mind enter the matter before it, without rushing away or ahead. That sounds simple. It is difficult to live.

Many of us approach work as something to get over with. Students want to hand in homework; adults want to finish a proposal; a meeting makes us think of its ending, work of going home. We scroll while eating and think about work while spending time with our children.

Things get completed, but the mind seldom enters them. Over time we become busy every day without retaining very much from what we do.

My child's project is a small example. Reading some Lu Xun carefully, noticing memorable people and stories, then deciding what to draw and write might produce something less polished than an online template. It would still be a work that genuinely belonged to the child.

More importantly, the process trains reading, understanding, judgment, expression, and concentration. Those abilities do not disappear when the assignment is handed in. They remain available for the next text, essay, exam, or unfamiliar problem.

![Each step becomes a cause: a child reads and writes attentively beside a new shoot](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-29-fufa-zhuanzhu-dangxia/images/02.png)

*Each step becomes a cause: a child reads and writes attentively beside a new shoot.*

I increasingly think that personal growth compounds because each way of doing something quietly shapes the person who will do the next thing. Careful reading today adds a little understanding tomorrow. Thinking through a difficult question builds patience for the next unfamiliar one. Learning to think before looking up an answer prepares the ground for independent judgment.

The reverse happens too. Cutting corners today makes bypassing the next problem more natural. Carelessness can compound as well.

This resembles one everyday way of understanding cause and effect. We often imagine consequences arriving only far in the future, but many are forming each day. The way we do one thing becomes a condition for how we do the next.

In school life, this may be simpler than we make it. Listen carefully, do the day's work, ask about what is unclear, read with attention, and examine mistakes after an exam. The grades that worry parents may gradually feel less frightening. They will fluctuate, but these habits can provide a sound foundation.

The child also need not live under constant pressure to reach a score or surpass someone else. Attention can return to what needs doing now: today's lesson, today's homework, today's unanswered question, and today's needed rest.

That can make life steadier. Anxiety often comes from chasing an outcome that has not arrived: falling grades, criticism, parental disappointment, or a future school place. The mind is already carried away before anything has happened.

Buddhist practice does not ask us to stop trying or pretend results are irrelevant. It helps put the sequence in order: **Attend to the causes first; allow outcomes to unfold through their conditions.**

Give the task your care, then meet the result. Not clinging to an outcome can return energy from worrying about it to the work itself.

![Give the task your care; allow the result its conditions: parent and child work quietly together](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-29-fufa-zhuanzhu-dangxia/images/03.png)

*Give the task your care; allow the result its conditions: parent and child work quietly together.*

I have come to like that simple phrase: **Give the task your care; allow the result its conditions.** This is not an excuse to do a half-hearted job. It means doing what is needed sincerely, then loosening the demand that the outcome must obey us. Study, take the exam attentively, and afterward learn from an eighty or a ninety. One score need not invalidate the child, and one success does not settle everything forever.

That is a stable way of learning, and to me more valuable than chasing a high score alone. School ends; the way a person meets a task may accompany them for decades.

University, work, and family continue to call for it: really listening, doing a job with care, handling a problem, and honoring a promise. These acts are so ordinary that we may never call them practice.

Yet the Diamond Sutra's opening does not ask us to leave ordinary life first. The Buddha still dresses, eats, walks, and washes. The change is that the mind stops treating what is before it casually.

If asked for a basic practice anyone can begin, I would now say: do the thing before you with care. A child can attend to today's class; an adult to today's work; a family member to the person beside them. We do not need an impressive spiritual state to start.

Ten or twenty years of such ordinary attention may produce more growth than we imagine. We need not do it solely for that compounded benefit. Fixating on the reward carries the mind away again.

Practice returns to today, to this moment, to this one thing. Plant the causes well. We need not ask every day when or in what form the fruit will arrive. Many good results grow gradually while we are giving our attention elsewhere.
