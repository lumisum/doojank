Since beginning to study Buddhism, I have noticed something curious. Alone, I can often understand the teachings: loosen my grip on gains and losses at work, or gradually let an unkind remark pass. At home, especially when raising a child, those same principles suddenly become difficult to live by.

The difficulty often lies between spouses as much as between parent and child. Both love the child and believe they want what is best. Their histories, habits of thought, and personalities may nevertheless produce completely different answers to what “best” means. Both pull hard in their chosen direction, leaving the child caught between them.

That made me wonder: the Buddha had a son, Rāhula. How did he teach him?

In the discourse advising Rāhula, the Buddha uses a mirror as an analogy. Asked what a mirror is for, Rāhula answers that it is for reflection. Before acting, one should examine whether the action will harm oneself, others, or both. One should keep examining during the action and reflect afterward. Harm calls for stopping, acknowledging, and correcting; actions that cause no harm and bring good results should be maintained.

![First look at yourself: Rāhula studies a mirror as the Buddha offers gentle guidance](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-26-fotuo-jiaoyu-luohouluo/images/01.png)

*First look at yourself: Rāhula studies a mirror as the Buddha offers gentle guidance.*

What interests me is the direction of this education, beyond any single rule. **He was training his son to exercise judgment, rather than remain forever dependent on a father who had the right answer.** Independence here does not mean doing whatever one wants. It means being able to observe, judge, and take responsibility even after leaving one's parents.

Many parents aim to make their child “listen to the right advice.” But if a child can only act well when an adult supplies the answer, what happens when the adult is absent? The mirror teaches a method: look before, during, and after acting. Judgment is gradually handed back to the child.

This does not mean parents stop caring. Age and ability matter; parents still need to uphold safety and boundaries and explain consequences. The difference is that boundaries should not become a reason to decide everything. Children can gradually participate in judgment and learn from actual outcomes.

Seen this way, disagreements between spouses become clearer. On the surface we debate methods; underneath, two adults may be clinging to their own identities. “I am the father, so my judgment must stand.” “I am the mother, so my experience must not be dismissed.” My knowledge, my method, my view of the child's future: none is necessarily wrong. Trouble begins when “I think this is better” becomes “You must acknowledge that I am right.”

The question of helping the child then becomes “Why don't you understand me?” and eventually “Which of us knows more about education?” The child slips out of the center. Two adults' versions of correctness remain.

![Has control slipped into goodwill? Parents discuss their child's education while the child listens](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-26-fotuo-jiaoyu-luohouluo/images/02.png)

*Has control slipped into goodwill? Parents discuss their child's education while the child listens.*

Clinging to an identity or view does not mean merely having opinions. It means making a provisional judgment untouchable. If I believe I am right, disagreement feels like rejection of me. If I say it is for the child, resistance feels like harm to the child. A question about a method becomes a threat to the self.

Both people may sincerely say, “I only want what is best for our child.” What Buddhism makes me examine is whether control has quietly entered that goodwill. Wanting a child to thrive and requiring the child to thrive in precisely the way I imagine are different things.

Returning to the mirror suggests another route: argue less about who is always right and examine actual results together. After six months, is the child more independent or dependent? More honest, or more skilled at reading the parents' moods? Better able to accept consequences, or more afraid of mistakes? If the results are poor, either person's method can change—including mine.

![Return judgment to the child: parents stay nearby while their child thinks independently](https://lumisum.github.io/doojank/articles/2026-09-26-fotuo-jiaoyu-luohouluo/images/03.png)

*Return judgment to the child: parents stay nearby while their child thinks independently.*

That is practice too. Sometimes the hardest thing to release is not “I might be wrong,” but “I am plainly right.” Non-clinging can mean allowing reality to keep testing our judgment, not turning a disagreeing spouse into a wrong person, and not needing to overpower them in order to hold a position.

I now see the Buddha's teaching as preparing a child who could eventually leave his father and still make thoughtful decisions. A child is not an extension of either parent's thinking, or a vehicle over whose steering wheel two adults compete. We can offer a method of judgment, the ability to accept consequences, and the habit of looking back after a mistake.

That mirror should face the parents first. Whenever we feel certain we are acting for the child's good, we might ask: **Am I helping my child form their own judgment, or trying to make them become my idea of the right person?**

Perhaps this is a crucial boundary between education and control. Family becomes a place of practice because those closest to us reveal where our minds are most tightly caught.

Scriptural reference: [MN 61, the discourse advising Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā (Chinese and English)](https://theravadacn.com/Sutta/Ambalatthikarahulovada2.htm). This essay offers my own interpretation and connections with daily life.
