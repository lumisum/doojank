While reading Chapter Seven of the Diamond Sutra, a sentence held my attention: “There is no fixed teaching that the Tathāgata can expound.”

At first, I understood it this way: language is a signpost. We name things to recognize them, propose concepts to discuss them, and build theories to understand them. But a name is not the thing itself, and a concept is not reality itself. I put this into a phrase for myself: **Use a name; do not cling to it.**

Then a practical question arose. If ordinary language points toward the world, what about mathematics? Mathematics is also a language, but a more precise and rigorous one. Ordinary speech says “getting faster”; mathematics defines velocity and acceleration. Ordinary speech says “circle”; mathematics provides a strict definition. Once axioms and rules are established, conclusions can even follow with complete certainty.

If Buddhism teaches that phenomena lack an independent, intrinsic nature, could these certain mathematical truths be the exception?

Gradually, I saw that **certainty and intrinsic existence are different questions.** A theorem follows necessarily because its axioms, definitions, and rules of inference have been specified. Its certainty still depends on those conditions. Being highly certain does not mean standing independently of every condition.

This makes the absence of an intrinsic nature more concrete for me. It does not imply that everything is vague or that regularities do not exist. It means that something cannot exist entirely by itself, apart from its conditions, relationships, and origins.

Consider an everyday analogy. A medicine helps someone recover, so they call it “the thing that heals.” Yet its ingredients may come from leaves, the leaves from a tree, and the tree from roots, soil, water, sunlight, and air. The soil depends on weathered rock, microorganisms, plant remains, water, and time. We can keep following the conditions further.

The medicine works, but it is difficult to identify a self-contained “essence of medicine” independent of every condition. Its form and effects arise through many conditions together.

**Effectiveness does not establish intrinsic existence. Neither does certainty.**

*Certainty does not equal intrinsic existence: a brass compass traces a circle over still water.*

Now consider π. It is precisely defined; it will not become 3.2 tomorrow. We can approach it through the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, or express it through series, integrals, and other mathematical structures. But does that certainty prove that π is an independently existing substance, detached from relationships? I do not think so. Our understanding of π takes place within mathematical definitions and relationships.

**Absolute certainty within relationships is different from independent existence outside all relationships.** I am using mathematics as an analogy here, rather than claiming mathematics and Buddhist teaching are the same thing.

Another question follows: if “all appearances lack an intrinsic nature,” does that statement itself become an absolute truth with an intrinsic nature? Would Buddhism have left itself an exception?

The Buddhist warning is precisely against clinging even to the absence of intrinsic nature. A medicine helps cure an illness; after recovery, insisting that it must always be right and can never be put down may turn the medicine into a problem. Emptiness can loosen our attachment to existence as fixed substance. Turning emptiness itself into an eternal substance merely renames the old attachment.

First we assume a thing has a fixed essence, then ask what it depends on. Seeing its conditions, we say it lacks intrinsic existence. If we then grip that concept tightly, it too becomes a tool we need to release.

![Arising through conditions: roots and water sustain plants growing from stone and soil](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-25-ruguo-fotuo-kanjian-pi/images/02.png)

*Arising through conditions: roots and water sustain plants growing from stone and soil.*

π offers another vivid reminder. Its decimal expansion continues: 3.1415926… There are more digits, and then more. This does not mean it lacks mathematical structure; it has a rigorous structure, but no final decimal digit. Buddhism and π are plainly different, yet they suggest something similar to me: calculating π leaves another digit; observing the world can leave another layer of conditions to examine.

Where did the medicine come from? Leaves. Where did the leaves come from? A tree. The tree? Soil, water, sunlight. Further questions reveal further conditions. The world begins to look less like a collection of isolated objects and more like a network of relationships.

![Beyond an answer, inquiry can continue: ripples spread from a stone through a mountain lake](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-25-ruguo-fotuo-kanjian-pi/images/03.png)

*Beyond an answer, inquiry can continue: ripples spread from a stone through a mountain lake.*

Perhaps this is why Buddhist inquiry feels inexhaustible to me. It need not keep inventing more elaborate theories. When we think we have reached the bottom, it asks: **What does this depend on?** If there is still an answer, what we have grasped is not independent of all conditions.

If the Buddha encountered π, I imagine he might be less interested in how many digits we had calculated than in why we long for a final digit: why do we want an ultimate answer beyond which no question remains?

Mathematics offers highly certain conclusions within rigorous systems, and physics keeps improving our understanding of the world. Both matter enormously. Buddhist teaching reminds me not to mistake precision for reality itself. A formula can hold, a theorem can be proved, π can be calculated, and medicine can heal. The medicine remains conditioned, and the formula remains a way of expressing our understanding.

I now read “no fixed teaching” as a reminder **not to turn an answer we have reached into the last answer we are allowed to question.** It need not mean that there are no regularities or truths.

Use a name; do not cling to it. Perhaps what deserves practice is less the endless search for a higher answer than the ability to keep looking, even when an answer is already in our hands.
