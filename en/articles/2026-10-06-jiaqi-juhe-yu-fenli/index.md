A reunion ends. The sadness afterward changes too. As our seven-day holiday draws to a close, the five days spent with family already feel brief. We live apart, yet the drive takes only three hours. Meeting every three or four months makes these small gatherings especially precious.

The first day always brings the strongest excitement. Months of missing one another give way to familiar people standing before us. Farewell stirs the heart again, this time with reluctance. Having someone to anticipate seeing, and someone you hate to leave, gives happiness a slightly bittersweet weight.

By the third or fourth day, ordinary life begins to reappear. Different habits, rhythms, and daily concerns become noticeable again. The excitement of arrival cannot remain unchanged. Affection is still there; the gathering has simply moved from the beauty we imagined into the texture of living together.

I began to understand how distance can make affection more visible. It gives our shared time an outline: waiting, meeting, then parting. Living together offers its own closeness. For us, however, the months between visits help us notice the few days together that we might otherwise take for granted.

After everyone leaves, the house becomes quiet, and loneliness comes easily. An autumn afternoon already seems to carry a hint of winter. Winter promises the year’s larger family reunion; anticipation arrives alongside sadness. The next gathering feels distant while the last remains vivid. That interval can feel helpless.

Inside that discomfort, I found an unspoken demand: let this arrangement stay. Let everyone remain here; let the warmth continue. But each person has a life to return to, and holidays end. Wanting to preserve that moment kept me comparing the quiet present with the days we had just shared.

This reunion made emptiness concrete. It depended on leave, time, transport, and our wish to meet. Those conditions came together, then changed. No gathering can sustain itself independently of what makes it possible. In that moment, the Buddha’s teaching felt true to life.

That understanding leaves affection meaningful. Those days together happened; farewell does not cancel their value. We can be attentive while together, keep caring afterward, and arrange another visit when possible. I can loosen the additional demand that love must prove itself by keeping everyone in the same place.

Then came another thought: even emptiness is empty. The hollow feeling also depends on conditions—the recent gathering, the sudden quiet, my memories and expectations. It changes too. I need not treat absence as an endless hole, or turn emptiness into a rule forbidding myself to miss anyone.

Seeing this, I felt the loneliness loosen and ordinary life return. I still look forward to our next reunion, knowing another farewell will follow. Both can be welcomed. Even “they have gone” need not become something I hold forever. **Be fully together when together; live fully when apart.**
