I increasingly feel that the Buddha's remarkable ability was not only awakening. He also had an extraordinary capacity to make the world intelligible.

That may sound odd. Does Buddhism not teach us to see through appearances, let go, and understand emptiness? By “defining,” I do not mean attaching permanent labels to everything. I mean distinguishing the layers of our tangled experience and seeing how they relate.

The Buddha did not merely say, “Life is difficult.” He taught suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Human experience was not left inside a single word, “I,” but examined through form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. Knowing the world was considered through the six sense faculties, their objects, and the corresponding consciousnesses. Afflictions were distinguished as greed, hatred, delusion, ignorance, and attachment; practice through the Eightfold Path, ethical conduct, concentration, wisdom, and the four foundations of mindfulness. Experiences that had been tangled together became distinguishable, with names and relationships.

This is more than a list of terms. It is a powerful cognitive ability: making the unclear visible.

A name does not replace an answer. Its usefulness is that it gives observation a place to begin. Anxiety, loss of control, and attachment to others' opinions may call for different responses. If we compress them all into “I feel bad,” we tend to respond to everything in the same way.

Often we are confused not because we feel nothing, but because we cannot say what is happening. “I am exhausted” might mean physical fatigue, anxiety, too many desires, or constant comparison. “I am irritated” might come from losing control, fixating on an outcome, or feeling judged. While those experiences remain tangled, they push us around. Distinguishing and naming them gives us a chance to see what we are actually facing.

For me, naming is a way of making confusion visible, rather than casually sticking on a label.

Recently, while building an AI agent, I focused on connecting business data, answering questions, calling tools, and improving accuracy. I was doing the work, but had no clear sense of where my agent belonged in the larger picture.

Then I heard someone distinguish three kinds of agents: those that assist with work, those that take over the work, and those entrusted with responsibility for outcomes. Suddenly I could locate what I was building. Mine was an assisting agent; the next questions concerned taking over work, and eventually taking responsibility. The system and code had not changed. A classification had simply helped me see where I stood and where I might go.

*Ideas on a desk become an organized set of levels, bringing a sense of direction.*

That is the power of a definition. Before it, I was doing things. After it, I understood my position and could see a possible next step. A cognitive breakthrough often comes from a new way of organizing facts, rather than merely knowing more facts.

I see something similar in the Buddha's teaching. He did not stop at “there is suffering,” but asked where it comes from, what forms it takes, what is cause and what is result, what can release it, and how. Unclear experience became a structure, and that structure became a path people could understand and practice.

An insight by itself is difficult to pass on. Something I realize today may be inaccessible to another person. If I can explain its parts and relationships, they have a route by which to understand it again for themselves. Perhaps this is one reason Buddhist teaching has continued across so much time: experience is accompanied by language and a framework that help others approach it.

There is a practical lesson here. We often rush to solve a problem without asking what kind of problem it is, how it differs from another, or what structure they share.

At work, is this a delivery task or a longer-term product issue? A temporary repair or a structural problem? In a relationship, does my discomfort concern a crossed boundary, excessive control, conflicting interests, or my own emotional projection? When I feel out of sorts, is it fatigue, anxiety, too many demands, or unclear direction? A distinction may not supply an answer immediately, but it makes the next step clearer.

Of course, reality does not always fit one category. A conflict can involve both boundaries and emotions. A project can require delivery now while revealing a deeper product problem. Classification should help us see the parts before deciding where to begin, rather than rush to a final verdict.

More definitions are not always better, and defining something is no reason to stop observing. Buddhism also warns against attachment to names and concepts. A useful classification can help us see now; if new circumstances exceed it, we should revise it. Treat a label as eternal truth, and it becomes another prison.

Defining the world can itself be a practice when it reduces confusion and brings clarity. First use a definition to see; then avoid being trapped by it. Together, these movements have real strength.

So when I ask what made the Buddha so extraordinary, I now add a personal answer: he could find structure in confusion, develop concepts from structure, understand conditions through those concepts, and finally avoid clinging to the concepts themselves. That is a remarkable level of understanding.

We cannot reproduce the Buddha. We can, however, learn to name a problem as well as solve it. When you can explain something clearly, you are often already closer to an answer.
