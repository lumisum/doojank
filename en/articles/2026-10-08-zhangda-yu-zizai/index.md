I just heard a line that stopped me: “At ease, we have not grown up. Grown up, we are no longer at ease.” Why do we often feel less free as our knowledge, abilities, and possessions grow? This is not an inevitable pattern for everyone. But it captures something I keep noticing in daily life: we become better at arranging our affairs while finding it harder to settle our minds.

Children cry, feel afraid, and experience disappointment. They are not naturally free of trouble. But they generally have not built the elaborate systems of evaluation adults live by. Rain, a bird, or a small stream may hold their delight for a long time. For adults, work, income, status, wealth, relationships, success, and failure increasingly organize life. We know what to pursue and carry more responsibility, yet acquiring more does not necessarily diminish our unease.

I have been wondering how this happens. Buddhist ideas about making distinctions and not clinging, considered alongside how we use our attention, suggest a path of understanding and practice. It cannot explain every form of suffering. But it helps me recognize one common additional drain: a task already demands effort, and the mind keeps demanding that it produce a particular result.

To clarify this, I will call judgments such as good and bad, beautiful and ugly, pleasant and painful, rich and poor, successful and unsuccessful, gaining and losing “paired distinctions.” This is a simplification for the discussion. It does not mean that Buddhist appearances come in only two kinds, or that the world consists of opposing extremes. Differences usually have degrees and depend on context. A choice that suits one situation may not suit another.

Some differences can be measured; others include judgments of value. We notice them and choose directions accordingly: a more reliable product, a child who understands something better, a life with less financial difficulty. Once a condition seems worth pursuing, we have a reason to move toward it and away from another. Seeking what is good, avoiding harm, preferring comfort to pain, and working to escape poverty can all participate in this process.

There is nothing inherently wrong with making such distinctions. We improve products because we can assess differences in performance and want to create something better. We study, work, and improve our lives because we recognize different possibilities and choose those we value. **Distinctions give us direction; attraction and avoidance give us motivation.** Feeling at ease need not require abandoning judgment, goals, or action.

A simple example helps locate where distress can begin. Someone aims to complete a task to 100 percent. After serious effort, they reach 90 percent. If they recognize both what has been achieved and what remains, they can investigate the problem and improve their next attempt. But if anything below 100 percent is unacceptable, even reaching 99 percent may leave them anxious, dissatisfied, or questioning their own worth.

The gap between goal and result can be the same, while the experience differs. What changes is how the gap is understood. It may be information that needs a response. Or it may become a reality the mind refuses to accept: “After all my effort, this cannot be the outcome.” The percentage itself is incidental. One person requires 90 percent, another 80. What can trap us is making a particular outcome an inner condition that must never fail to be met.

This does not mean objective standards can simply be lowered. Safety requirements, quality standards, and contractual commitments are not satisfied because we achieved 90 percent. We must acknowledge shortfalls, correct defects, and accept responsibility. But “this work is not yet acceptable” differs from “I cannot accept this shortfall, therefore I have no value.” We can hold work to demanding standards without fastening the result permanently to our evaluation of ourselves.

Nor does achievement guarantee ease. Before obtaining something, we suffer over not having it. With part of it, we suffer over not having enough. Once we have it all, we may fear losing it. Sometimes the same demand runs through each state: what I want must appear as expected, reach the expected level, and remain. The goal that once oriented my actions has become the only condition under which I allow myself peace.

Reality does not unfold entirely according to personal intention. An outcome depends on abilities, methods, resources, other people's choices, and changing circumstances. Illness, injury, loss, and material pressures can cause real suffering. These cannot all be reduced to “you are too attached.” What I want to recognize is the additional distress that can grow when the mind keeps insisting reality must match its expectations.

This helps me understand one part of the unease of growing up. As knowledge, relationships, and responsibilities expand, so do the standards by which we measure ourselves. Income must reach a certain level. Children should develop a certain way. Careers must not fall behind. Other people must understand our contribution. Comparison and measurement begin as tools for understanding and improving reality, then can become demands that must be fulfilled. A gap that could guide action instead becomes a recurring reason to blame ourselves.

From here, the Buddhist idea of not clinging becomes more concrete for me. Differences do not disappear because I decide to let go. Success and failure, gaining and losing still occur. These appearances are also not permanent, independently existing things; they emerge through conditions. What I can practice is changing my relationship with them: seeing, judging, and acting without demanding that a particular state remain forever.

The Diamond Sutra speaks of giving rise to the mind without dwelling on anything. The practice addressed in the scripture is richer than my everyday interpretation. One reminder I take from it is to attend to both parts: not dwelling, and still giving rise to the mind. As the demand for a guaranteed outcome loosens, I can continue to act carefully, accept responsibility, and care for others. **Recognize distinctions in the world, take action in your affairs, and practice a mind that does not cling.** This describes a direction I hope to move toward, not a state I claim to have achieved.

Understanding the idea still leaves the question of how to live it. Two familiar Chinese sayings offer a starting point: do what lies within your power and accept what lies beyond it; work on the causes and let the outcome follow its conditions. I began to think about them through attention. The time and energy we can give a task are limited. Choosing what to do also means choosing where that finite attention goes.

I call my practical summary the “Attention-to-Causes Principle.” The Chinese name, 注意力相因原则, joins the desired appearance or state with the causes that can help bring it about. It is my term, not an established Buddhist expression or a scientifically proven law. It is a reminder for arranging life: **Let the goal set direction, give attention to its causes, and use the outcome to evaluate action without clinging to it.**

The goal answers where I am heading. The causes answer what I can do now. Doing my part means investing attention as effectively as I can in the work and conditions that may support the goal. Working on causes means making that investment concrete, step by step, as events develop. Results matter because they reveal whether direction or method needs adjustment. They are not guaranteed, however, and effort is one condition among many.

For example, if you want to build a good software product, you need performance, quality, and delivery targets. Once work begins, attention can return to understanding requirements, designing the architecture, implementing code, testing, and improving. Analyze the requirements carefully. Write the code carefully. Investigate problems when they appear. Adjust direction when feedback calls for it. Each step helps create conditions for a better result.

The same working time can also be occupied by another activity: repeatedly wondering whether the project will succeed, whether your manager will be satisfied, or whether competitors will get ahead. Some of these questions need investigation. Clarifying evaluation criteria, analyzing competitive risks, and planning responses are legitimate work. But if no new information emerges, no decision develops, and the same worry merely repeats, it may no longer be helping the project.

The distinction is between useful thinking and endless circling. Planning, risk assessment, and necessary review can all count as working on causes; they need not involve immediate physical activity. The question is whether attention makes the problem clearer, produces a choice, or creates conditions for the next step. **Thinking about a goal is not the same as attending to the actions that can achieve it.** Imagining success a hundred times may contribute less than resolving one concrete obstacle to it.

Helping a child learn offers another example. We may hope for better grades. But once we sit down together, attention can go to what the child does not understand, which explanation might help, and which practice would build understanding. Repeatedly asking why the score was not perfect does something different. In the same hour, one approach may address a difficulty with the material. The other, if it consists only of expressing dissatisfaction, can leave that difficulty unresolved while adding tension to the relationship.

Working on causes is therefore more than encouragement to try harder. It is a way of allocating attention. It includes rest, asking for help, and revising an unreasonable goal. It does not require every minute to produce an output or turn life into a competition for maximum efficiency. If someone is too exhausted to continue effectively, genuine rest may support the next action better than forcing themselves onward.

Letting the outcome follow its conditions, meanwhile, means allowing a result to become feedback. If a task reaches only 90 percent, acknowledge that reality. Examine what needs improvement, take responsibility where required, and adjust the next attempt when circumstances change. This continues the work on causes. Accepting the facts lets us begin from where we actually are rather than from the imagined place where we believe we should have been.

If the necessary review is complete, no new information is available, and no further action can currently be taken, repeatedly asking why the result was not perfect can consume attention needed for the next effort. It cannot rewrite the past and may create no new conditions. Accepting what lies beyond our power means recognizing that we cannot fully control outcomes. Letting them follow their conditions means respecting reality rather than demanding it fulfill every expectation.

This does not require quietly accepting mistreatment. An unfulfilled promise or unfair arrangement can still call for discussion, challenge, help, or a change of circumstances. Responsibility does not disappear under the language of letting go. Take the actions available, and face honestly what those actions cannot presently change. Accepting that something has happened does not mean approving of it or ruling out future change.

The principle can enter ordinary tasks. Before acting, clarify direction. During the work, give attention to concrete steps. When results appear, evaluate and adjust, then decide what comes next. If an effort is finished, allow it to finish. If continuing is worthwhile, begin again. Some consequences still need prolonged attention, and letting go rarely happens in an instant. But we do not have to add the demand that the past produce a different version of itself.

Nor must we suppress distress the moment it arises. Disappointment needs acknowledgment, and sadness sometimes needs time. We can gradually notice what we are repeatedly holding onto, then return some attention to caring for ourselves or addressing the situation. A mind that does not cling to outcomes, with attention given to causes, is a direction of practice. It should not become another ruler with which to punish ourselves for still feeling sad.

Returning to the opening line, the ease worth seeking in adulthood cannot mean becoming unaware again. We have developed judgment and carry real responsibilities. Knowing better and worse, we can choose. Recognizing strengths and weaknesses, we can improve. Understanding success and failure, we can still strive. What can gradually loosen is the demand that one particular outcome must occur before our lives are allowed to have value.

Along this path, distinctions help establish direction, attraction and avoidance motivate action, and action participates in creating conditions. When the mind turns an outcome into a requirement that cannot loosen, additional distress can arise. Not clinging offers an opportunity to reduce that constraint. It will not remove every difficulty. But it may return energy spent on repeated worry and self-blame to something worth doing.

This is my current understanding of the Attention-to-Causes Principle. The goal can remain ahead without becoming a weight we must carry on our backs. Choose something worthwhile and attend carefully to the steps it requires. Meet the result honestly. Continue where needed, change course where appropriate, and gradually release what can be released. Doing our part is an effective investment of attention. Accepting what lies beyond our power is a clear acknowledgment of the actual outcome.

Working on causes brings me closer to the life happening now. Letting outcomes follow their conditions means one result need not repeatedly become the beginning of another round of distress. As adults, perhaps we cannot recover a child's ease. But we can learn to see the world more fully, continue to act seriously, and avoid trapping the mind inside a single result. Doing the present task well is the practice I am willing to begin today.
