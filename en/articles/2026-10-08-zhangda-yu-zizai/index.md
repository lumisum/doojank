You keep working toward a goal, yet the result falls short of what you expected. Sometimes you miss by so little that regret and disappointment arrive together. You can see the effort you put in and the gap that remains, but you cannot push the result that final step forward. The task has reached a stopping point. Your mind keeps asking: why couldn’t it have gone just a little better?

A line I just heard captures this tension: “At ease, we have not grown up. Grown up, we are no longer at ease.” Children also cry, feel afraid, and experience disappointment. But they generally have not built the elaborate evaluation systems adults live by. Rain, a bird, or a stream can delight them for a long time. As we grow up, work, income, status, relationships, success, and failure organize more of our days. We become clearer about what we want and less willing to accept an outcome that falls short.

Buddhist ideas about distinctions and non-attachment, considered alongside how we use attention, make this drain easier to see. The task demands effort. The mind adds a command: after this much work, the result must turn out as expected. The project must advance, the outcome must arrive, and other people must recognize it. A goal accumulates more and more demands that are not allowed to go unmet.

Here I use “paired distinctions” as shorthand for judgments such as good and bad, beautiful and ugly, pleasant and painful, rich and poor, success and failure, gaining and losing. Reality has degrees and depends on context; it cannot be divided into two extremes. This shorthand helps trace how we move from recognizing differences to choosing a direction—and then to demanding that a particular condition occur.

Some differences can be measured; others include judgments of value. We notice them and choose directions accordingly: a more reliable product, a child who understands something better, a life with less financial difficulty. Once a condition seems worth pursuing, we have a reason to move toward it and away from another. Seeking what is good, avoiding harm, preferring comfort to pain, and working to escape poverty can all participate in this process.

There is nothing inherently wrong with making such distinctions. We improve products because we can assess differences in performance and want to create something better. We study, work, and improve our lives because we recognize different possibilities and choose those we value. **Distinctions give us direction; attraction and avoidance give us motivation.** Feeling at ease need not require abandoning judgment, goals, or action.

A simple example helps locate where distress can begin. Someone aims to complete a task to 100 percent. After serious effort, they reach 90 percent. If they recognize both what has been achieved and what remains, they can investigate the problem and improve their next attempt. But if anything below 100 percent is unacceptable, even reaching 99 percent may leave them anxious, dissatisfied, or questioning their own worth.

A gap treated as information points toward the next step. A gap treated as something that must never exist keeps hurting. “Ten percent remains. How do I improve?” can move the work forward. “After all that effort, how can ten percent still remain?” can keep the mind circling. The percentage is incidental: the same trap exists at 90 or 80. When peace must wait for a particular result, the mind is held by that result.

Objective standards still apply. Safety requirements, quality standards, and contractual commitments are not met by reaching 90 percent. Fix defects and take responsibility. Unacceptable work calls for identifying what is missing and improving the conditions; repeatedly blaming ourselves adds another drain. Standards can remain demanding without every shortfall destabilizing the mind.

Reverse the question: if distress came only from not obtaining something, obtaining it should end the distress. Yet before we have it, we worry about its absence. With some of it, we worry about not having enough. With all of it, we may fear losing it. The shared demand is that the condition we want must appear, be sufficient, and last. **Getting something can satisfy a desire without releasing the demand that things must go our way.** As long as that demand remains, distress has another way back in.

Reality does not unfold entirely according to personal intention. An outcome depends on abilities, methods, resources, other people's choices, and changing circumstances. Illness, injury, loss, and material pressures can cause real suffering. These cannot all be reduced to “you are too attached.” What I want to recognize is the additional distress that can grow when the mind keeps insisting reality must match its expectations.

This helps me understand one part of the unease of growing up. As knowledge, relationships, and responsibilities expand, so do the standards by which we measure ourselves. Income must reach a certain level. Children should develop a certain way. Careers must not fall behind. Other people must understand our contribution. Comparison and measurement begin as tools for understanding and improving reality, then can become demands that must be fulfilled. A gap that could guide action instead becomes a recurring reason to blame ourselves.

Non-attachment has a practical meaning here. Success and failure still occur; gains and losses still happen. They arise through conditions and change with those conditions. We can recognize differences, choose a direction, and act carefully while dropping the demand that one state must permanently guarantee our peace. A goal can remain ahead without holding the mind there.

The Diamond Sutra speaks of giving rise to the mind without dwelling on anything. Applied to everyday affairs, the direction I take from it is clear: keep the mind from being trapped by results and continue to do the work carefully. Both parts matter. Releasing attachment leaves action, responsibility, and care intact. **Recognize distinctions in the world, take action in your affairs, and practice a mind that does not cling.** This ability is built through actual choices.

Understanding the idea still leaves the question of how to live it. Two familiar Chinese sayings offer a starting point: do what lies within your power and accept what lies beyond it; work on the causes and let the outcome follow its conditions. I began to think about them through attention. The time and energy we can give a task are limited. Choosing what to do also means choosing where that finite attention goes.

I call this practical method the **Attention-to-Cause Principle (ACP)**. It is my summary of how to approach everyday action. “Toward causes” emphasizes a change in direction: moving finite attention away from repeatedly demanding a result and toward the concrete actions and conditions that can produce it. Its core definition is: **Set your direction through goals, direct your attention toward causes, learn from outcomes, but never become attached to results.**

The goal answers where I am heading. The causes answer what I can do now. Doing my part means investing attention as effectively as I can in the work and conditions that may support the goal. Working on causes means making that investment concrete, step by step, as events develop. Results matter because they reveal whether direction or method needs adjustment. They are not guaranteed, however, and effort is one condition among many.

For example, if you want to build a good software product, you need performance, quality, and delivery targets. Once work begins, attention can return to understanding requirements, designing the architecture, implementing code, testing, and improving. Analyze the requirements carefully. Write the code carefully. Investigate problems when they appear. Adjust direction when feedback calls for it. Each step helps create conditions for a better result.

The same hours can be occupied by another activity: repeatedly wondering whether the project will succeed, whether your manager will be satisfied, or whether competitors will get ahead. Clarifying criteria, researching risks, and planning responses create conditions for progress. But considering the same question ten times without new information, a decision, or a next step does not become productive investment merely because you kept thinking about the task.

**You think you are working toward a result. Sometimes you are only worrying about it.** Look at what the thinking leaves behind: a clearer problem, a decision, or a next step. Planning, risk assessment, and necessary review work on causes. Repeated worry that makes no progress consumes the attention those activities need. Imagining success a hundred times cannot substitute for resolving one concrete obstacle to it.

Helping a child learn offers another example. We may hope for better grades. But once we sit down together, attention can go to what the child does not understand, which explanation might help, and which practice would build understanding. Repeatedly asking why the score was not perfect does something different. In the same hour, one approach may address a difficulty with the material. The other, if it consists only of expressing dissatisfaction, can leave that difficulty unresolved while adding tension to the relationship.

Working on causes is therefore more than encouragement to try harder. It is a way of allocating attention. It includes rest, asking for help, and revising an unreasonable goal. It does not require every minute to produce an output or turn life into a competition for maximum efficiency. If someone is too exhausted to continue effectively, genuine rest may support the next action better than forcing themselves onward.

Letting outcomes follow their conditions means returning a result to its role as feedback. If the task reached 90 percent, begin there. Analyze the gap, accept responsibility, and adjust the next attempt. All require an accurate starting point. Repeating “it should have been 100 percent” moves that point no further. Acceptance matters because it restores our ability to deal with what actually happened.

Reason backward from the outcome again. If repeated worry improved the situation, it would produce new information, a decision, or an action. When the necessary review is complete and no new conditions have emerged, circling around why the result fell short takes attention from the next attempt. **The harder you try to make the past turn out differently, the more you risk giving it the energy needed to change what comes next.** Accepting what lies beyond our power acknowledges the many conditions shaping results. Letting outcomes follow those conditions releases the demand that they fulfill every expectation.

This does not require quietly accepting mistreatment. An unfulfilled promise or unfair arrangement can still call for discussion, challenge, help, or a change of circumstances. Responsibility does not disappear under the language of letting go. Take the actions available, and face honestly what those actions cannot presently change. Accepting that something has happened does not mean approving of it or ruling out future change.

The principle can enter ordinary tasks. Before acting, clarify direction. During the work, give attention to concrete steps. When results appear, evaluate and adjust, then decide what comes next. If an effort is finished, allow it to finish. If continuing is worthwhile, begin again. Some consequences still need prolonged attention, and letting go rarely happens in an instant. But we do not have to add the demand that the past produce a different version of itself.

Disappointment needs acknowledgment, and sadness needs time. Returning attention to causes does not mean ordering yourself to feel happy immediately. Notice the emotion, care for yourself, and address what can be addressed. Turning “I must let go immediately” into another demand repeats the old mechanism. If practice becomes another test we must pass, even letting go becomes a burden.

Returning to the opening line, the ease worth seeking in adulthood cannot mean becoming unaware again. We have developed judgment and carry real responsibilities. Knowing better and worse, we can choose. Recognizing strengths and weaknesses, we can improve. Understanding success and failure, we can still strive. What can gradually loosen is the demand that a particular outcome must occur before the mind is allowed to settle.

Another reversal concerns the order of events. We say, “Once the outcome improves, I can settle down and get on with the work.” Yet the work needs doing now. Making peace depend entirely on the future keeps drawing attention away from the present. Loosening that dependence creates room for action. Distinctions establish direction, attraction and avoidance provide motivation, action creates conditions, and non-attachment reduces the mind’s added entanglement with results.

ACP keeps the goal ahead and brings attention back to the work at hand. Choose a worthwhile task and attend carefully to its steps. Meet the result honestly. Continue where needed, adjust where appropriate, and begin releasing what can be released. Doing our part is an effective investment of attention. Accepting what lies beyond our power means acknowledging the actual outcome clearly. You can work toward the trophy without carrying it on your back.

Working on causes gives our limited attention to the conditions we can help create. Letting outcomes follow their conditions keeps one result from repeatedly starting another round of distress. Adult ease is rebuilt through understanding circumstances and taking responsibility. Choose a direction, do the work carefully, evaluate the result, and return attention to the next step. Ease does not have to wait until every goal is achieved. The stone at your feet can be laid now.
