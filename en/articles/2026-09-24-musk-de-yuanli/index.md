While trying to understand the Buddhist idea of the power of aspiration, someone unexpectedly came to mind: Elon Musk.

I am not saying he is Buddhist or judging his spiritual attainment. I am borrowing a Buddhist perspective to ask how someone can keep very different ventures moving toward directions worth pursuing. For me, aspiration is part of what makes this interesting.

SpaceX explores space; Tesla has expanded from electric vehicles into energy, AI, and robotics; Neuralink explores brain–computer interfaces. These are very different fields, so it would be too simple to say they all serve one aspiration. Each venture has its own larger direction. A rocket is a means toward a vision for space, rather than the vision itself. An electric car is a vehicle for changes in energy and transport. Products and routes can change while a broader aspiration persists.

This makes me think of vectors. A vector has direction as well as magnitude. Our daily actions are like small vectors: reading a book, learning a skill, meeting someone, or moving a project forward. Each has force. What matters is where those forces eventually point.

If we head east today, west tomorrow, and north the next day, great effort may leave us with very little net movement. Years of busyness can amount to surprisingly little distance. Different paths, however, can accumulate when they point broadly toward a worthwhile direction. The effect may be hard to see after a year, yet substantial after five or ten.

That is one practical way I now understand aspiration: it does not simply make you exert more force. It keeps your effort focused over time.

This also speaks to a question I struggled with while considering Wang Yangming's unity of knowing and acting. Why do we know something matters and still fail to begin? Why do we begin, only to stop after days or months? Why do so many principles we understand fail to change us?

I developed my own four-part account of moving something forward: understanding, judgment, action, and verification through feedback. It helps explain how to work on a particular task. Aspiration operates at another level.

It is not one of those four steps. Within an aspiration, understanding, judgment, action, and verification can change repeatedly. Old knowledge can be overturned, methods replaced, and action even paused. If our choices still return to the larger aspiration, the new approach is another way of refocusing.

Aspiration may therefore answer why someone keeps acting and how many different actions remain oriented, rather than merely how to act. That is what interests me about Musk.

Borrowing Buddhist vocabulary loosely, I might even describe him as having a strong capacity for insight. This is not a claim about Buddhist attainment. I mean that he seems able to identify a larger problem and organize technology, capital, people, and sustained effort around it.

Tesla illustrates this: electric vehicles, energy, increasingly intelligent systems, and robotics can be understood within broader visions even as the products change. The interesting question is less why he keeps making cars than how actions at different stages can continue to converge.

Aspiration provides direction; action gives it expression. It does not prescribe every step, but keeps informing our choices. That is when the phrase acquires practical force.

Our problem is often not inactivity, but actions that cancel one another out. We exercise for a week, decide to learn English, begin posting online, then switch to another way of earning money. Each choice may be reasonable. Together they may not yet produce much cumulative force.

Aspiration changes the alignment among countless actions, rather than the intensity of a single effort. Execution determines how far an arrow flies. Aspiration determines where thousands of arrows are aimed.

This helps me understand why Buddhist practice can emphasize aspiration while also warning against attachment to outcomes. Not clinging to a result does not mean refusing results. It means returning attention from the distant outcome to today's small vector: is this choice still pointing broadly in that direction?

When many choices remain aligned, knowledge, ability, resources, experience, and judgment accumulate. No outcome is guaranteed. Yet sustained investment of limited time and energy in a worthwhile direction can improve the chances of a good result.

Aspiration then becomes less mysterious. It is a source of direction that allows small actions to build into a combined force over time. We can have several aspirations, and different ones at different stages of life. The point is not to face only one way forever, but to reduce cancellation and increase accumulation within something worth pursuing.

Not clinging to results does not mean failing to create them. Letting go of fixation may help us return to actual action, again and again. One arrow is small; thousands broadly aligned are something else.

Aspiration does not merely make a person work harder. It helps their efforts begin to work together.
