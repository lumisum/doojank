Once a child enters middle school, many parents face a practical question: should we still take them to school and collect them every day?

In primary school, that care is natural. Young children have limited experience crossing roads, taking buses, and handling unexpected situations. By twelve or thirteen, however, parents may begin wondering whether it is time to let them make the journey independently.

Independence does not arrive suddenly on an eighteenth birthday. Keeping time, choosing a route, and handling small difficulties are themselves part of growing up. If the school run takes an hour each day, letting go may return an hour to the parent and give the child a space of their own.

The reasoning is straightforward; the first morning alone may feel very different. Have they arrived? Will they look at their phone while crossing? What if they miss the stop? What if something happens? You understand the need for autonomy, yet want to accompany them a little further. The mind does not know where to settle.

*Letting go does not mean abandoning responsibility: a parent watches a child leave along a safe pavement.*

Reading Chapter Two of the Diamond Sutra, I noticed a resemblance to Subhūti's questions about how those who aspire to awakening should abide and how they should bring their minds under control.

Those questions can sound remote. In ordinary life I hear something immediate: what is my mind relying on now, and how do I meet the worry that keeps returning?

Often the difficult part is not knowing what to do. It is recognizing that an old way needs to change before we have learned to live with the new one. We understand that children need independence, but years of parenting have taught us that we feel safe when they remain nearby and when arrangements stay in our hands.

The mind had a familiar resting place: I drive, collect, arrange, and confirm; therefore I feel secure. Letting go unsettles that place. We seek substitutes: checking location, asking whether they have arrived, imagining trouble after a few minutes without a reply. The parent stayed home, but the mind followed the child all the way to school.

![The mind is still following the child: a parent watches from a window, phone close at hand](https://lumisum.github.io/wulai/articles/2026-09-27-fotuo-rang-jiazhang-meitian-duo-yixiaoshi/images/02.png)

*The mind is still following the child: a parent watches from a window, phone close at hand.*

What is hard to release is more than a journey. It is the belief that enough control will make everything safe. The mind can cling to work, identity, reputation, a child's future, or the image of being a responsible father or good mother. When these supports loosen, a new question appears: without controlling everything, where can I find security?

That makes Subhūti's question close to home. Seeing attachment reveals the tension between wanting change and not yet knowing how to change. We want to trust the child's judgment and still fear injury. Anxiety often appears between a loosening old habit and a new way not yet established.

I no longer want to understand bringing the mind under control as forcibly suppressing thought. A parent's worry is normal. Buddhism does not require indifference or using “let things take their course” to avoid responsibility. We can walk the route together, teach traffic rules and responses to difficulties, judge readiness according to age and actual conditions, and agree on an arrival message.

That is responsibility. After preparing, worry and the urge to regain control may remain. We can first recognize them rather than demand instant calm. Concern arises from love, and also from our fear of uncertainty. When the necessary preparation is done, the child can begin traveling a stretch that belongs to them.

We are caring for the child while also attending to our own mind. **Working with the mind need not mean erasing a thought. It can mean seeing the thought without immediately following it.**

Both child and parent are growing. The child gradually becomes able to protect themselves. The parent gradually shifts from managing everything to teaching judgment and allowing the child to use it. This is a stepwise transfer of choice and responsibility within safe boundaries.

*Learning not to cling: the parent returns to their day as the child continues toward school.*

It might look as though the Buddha has given parents an extra hour a day. That hour is only one result. The deeper change is returning a small portion of the child's life to the child, while facing our own question: can I be at ease when I loosen my grip?

Subhūti's questions can also belong to a child's first independent school journey and a parent's first genuine act of letting go. We imagine awakening as suddenly understanding something. What is often harder comes afterward: the familiar self no longer quite fits, and the new way of being has not fully grown.

Chapter Two opens a door at that moment. The answer that follows does not offer the mind a stronger place to cling to. It points toward gradually learning **not to cling to any such resting place.**
